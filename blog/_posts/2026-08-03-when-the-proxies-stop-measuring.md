[Terence Tao](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Terence_Tao)'s public lecture at the [International Congress of Mathematicians in Philadelphia](https://www.mathunion.org/icm/icm-2026), delivered on [24 July 2026](https://www.simonsfoundation.org/2026/07/01/free-public-events-will-bring-the-wonder-of-mathematics-to-philadelphia/), sidesteps the capability question altogether. He assumes what he calls a Working Hypothesis, that a reasonably strong form of the capability conjecture holds, and asks what follows *conditionally*, on the grounds that the argument about capability is orthogonal to the argument about values. That is the right methodological move, and this post follows it: nothing below depends on how good these systems turn out to be.

It is the second of the two mechanisms this series runs on, and it is independent of the first. The first, set out in [Generation is hard, checking is cheap](/blog/generation-is-hard-checking-is-cheap/), was about tasks. Inside a formal system checking is decidable while finding a proof is not, so verification is cheap and generation is expensive; but the asymmetry holds only where a formal criterion exists, and in informal mathematics the checker is a person reading a paper for a week. Where generation is cheap, machine output is therefore absorbed without friction wherever checking is cheap and causes institutional trouble wherever it is not. The mechanism here is about institutions instead of tasks, and it would bite even if every machine-generated proof arrived with a certificate attached.

This post is the core of the argument. It proposes a mechanism for something the lecture states as an observation, follows the consequences through the optimistic and the pessimistic cases, marks the boundary of what a proof-centred diagnosis can reach, and ends with the part of the situation that no institutional analysis describes, which is what the change costs the people living through it.

## Why the goals come apart

The load-bearing claim of Tao's lecture is that mathematics has many goals, among them solving problems, building theory, understanding the world, building a community, training successors, contributing to shared knowledge and making work of lasting aesthetic value; that these have historically been positively correlated, so that one could serve as a proxy for the others; and that heavy optimisation against AI-friendly metrics may pull them apart.

The lecture states this as an observation. I think there is a clean mechanism underneath it, and having the mechanism sharpens the policy conclusion. But before the mechanism it is worth saying why that list of goals is more than a list, because there is a philosophical literature on exactly this point and the argument of this series has been quietly working inside it.

The philosophy of mathematical practice, whose founding figures are Lakatos and Tymoczko, begins from the observation that proofs have epistemic virtues other than correctness. Paolo Mancosu's formulation is the one to remember: if mathematicians cared only whether results were true, it would be hard to explain why, having proved something, they so often go on to prove it again. They do so because different proofs have different virtues. Explanation is the most discussed of them, the difference between a proof that convinces you a theorem is true and one that shows you why it is true. The standard catalogue also includes purity, rigour, visualizability, depth, simplicity, fruitfulness, beauty and transferability.

That reframes the question of what a machine optimising for correctness is actually optimising for. Correctness is one virtue among many, and it is the only one on the list that can be checked mechanically, which is precisely why it is the one benchmarks measure and the one training signals reward. Every other virtue there is defined by its effect on a reader. A proof cannot be explanatory, or deep, or beautiful, to nobody.

The sharpest illustration is purity of methods, the demand that a theorem be proved using only resources belonging to the area in which it is stated. [Atle Selberg](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Atle_Selberg) received the [Fields Medal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fields_Medal) in 1950 with a citation naming, among his other work, the [elementary proof](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Elementary_proof) of the [prime number theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prime_number_theorem) that he and [Erdős](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paul_Erd%C5%91s) had obtained. The theorem had been proved half a century earlier by complex-analytic means. The discipline's highest honour went, in part, for proving something already proved, because the new proof possessed a virtue the old one lacked. That is a flat refutation of benchmark logic and of any scoring scheme in which a problem leaves the board once an answer to it exists. Where the measure of progress is problems closed, purity is invisible, and so is most of what mathematicians have historically rewarded.

That has a consequence for how the discipline's values and aesthetics survive, and it is less comfortable than the usual formulation of that worry. Aesthetic judgment is the most tacit layer of the practice. It is not written down, it is not taught explicitly, and it is transmitted almost entirely by apprenticeship: by watching somebody experienced react to a proof and gradually acquiring the reactions. That makes it the most vulnerable part of what mathematicians know to the depreciation mechanism described later in this post, not the safest. A corpus preserves theorems. It does not preserve the reactions.

Two things are also worth separating that the phrase about centring human values tends to run together. One is preserving what mathematicians presently happen to like, which is a matter of taste and has changed repeatedly. The other is maintaining the standards that make the subject cumulative, so that results can be built upon rather than merely accumulated. Only the second is at stake in anything argued here, and conflating them makes it harder to tell which disruptions are genuine losses and which are only changes.

## Invented or discovered

There is an older question underneath this one, and it is worth surfacing because the argument of this series quietly depends on how it is answered. Is mathematics discovered or invented? On the [Platonist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Philosophy_of_mathematics#Platonism) reading, mathematical objects and truths exist independently of us, so that if the universe ceased tomorrow the theorems would remain true and the mathematician's work is a matter of finding what was already there. On the constructivist or conventionalist reading, mathematics is a human artefact, a system of rules no more independent of its players than chess or football, and its truths would vanish with the people who made them.

Nothing in the recent evidence bears on that question, and nobody should expect it to. Both positions absorb any capability result whatever: a Platonist reads the August announcements as a better instrument trained on the same landscape, a conventionalist as a machine playing our invented game faster than we can. A question that survived incompleteness and undecidability intact will not be settled by a benchmark.

It matters here for a different reason. The central claim of this series is that a literature of correct, unread, uncanonicalised results is a serious loss, and how serious it is depends on which reading one holds. If the truths are out there, an unread theorem is an accurate map that nobody has consulted: regrettable, wasteful, but the territory is unaffected and the map keeps its validity indefinitely. If mathematics is instead constituted by a practice, then a theorem's content is its role in that practice, and marks on a page that nobody has read and nothing has been built on are not yet mathematics at all. This series has been assuming the second, and it should say so. The Lakatos material above, in which definitions are negotiated through failed proofs, and the Wittgensteinian thought that a proof creates a concept rather than reporting a fact, both belong to that side. A convinced Platonist could accept every institutional argument made here and reply, not unreasonably, that what is being described is a problem of human access rather than a problem in mathematics.

## Joint production and its collapse

*The goals were correlated because they shared a single binding constraint: expert attention.* Each was produced by the same scarce input, and the production processes were bundled. You could not solve the problem without understanding the area; you could not understand the area without reading and digesting it; you trained students by having them watch you do it; the survey got written because writing it was how you organised your own thinking. Solving a problem *produced* understanding, community and training as joint products, at no extra cost. Optimising any one of them therefore moved all of them, and the proxies worked. This is an interpretation rather than a measurement, and it should be flagged as such: nobody has quantified the correlation between these goals, and the indicators proposed at the end test the mechanism's predicted consequences rather than the mechanism itself. An alternative reading, harder to distinguish than one would like, is that the goals were never as correlated as the profession's self-description suggests, and that what is changing is only how visible the gaps are.

AI relaxes the constraint on exactly one node of that network, generation, and leaves the others where they were. Once joint production breaks, the correlation has no reason to survive. A solved problem no longer certifies that anyone understood the area; a publication no longer certifies that a student was trained; a citation no longer certifies that anything was digested.

Two consequences follow at once. The first is that the scarce factor moves to verification and digestion, and the incentives must move with it. This is not a moral appeal but a bottleneck argument: if generation is cheap and digestion is expensive, throughput is set by digestion, and every hour of prestige allocated to generation is wasted. It is why the lecture's recommendation, to de-emphasise being first to a proof and elevate exposition, publication and canonicalisation, is the correct response rather than merely a pious one. It is also the recommendation the profession will find hardest to act on, since refereeing and exposition are unpaid, low-status and invisible on a curriculum vitae.

There is a philosophical account of what exactly gets lost if that reweighting does not happen, and it is worth having because it is more precise than the complaint that results go unread. [Imre Lakatos](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Imre_Lakatos)'s [*Proofs and Refutations*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Proofs_and_Refutations) reconstructs the history of Euler's polyhedron formula as a dialectic in which a conjecture meets a counterexample and the *statement* is what changes: hypotheses get added, definitions get patched, monsters get barred, and the theorem that survives is not the theorem anyone set out to prove. On that account definitions are not fixed in advance but negotiated through failed proofs, and digestion is not a tidying-up operation performed on a finished result. It is the process by which a result acquires its final content. A pipeline that mass-produces verified proofs of unexamined statements skips that process altogether, and what it yields is not a compressed version of ordinary mathematics but a different thing wearing the same notation.

Lakatos also supplies the most useful practical corollary in this series, and it converges with advice reached here from a quite different direction. If mathematics advances by refutation as much as by proof, the machine's most valuable role may be as refuter rather than prover. That is exactly what [Alex Kontorovich](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alex_Kontorovich) found when the dual search on his [prime number theorem formalisation project](https://github.com/AlexKontorovich/PrimeNumberTheoremAnd), which looks for a proof of a goal and for a proof of its negation at the same time, kept disproving his own intermediate statements and exposing hypotheses he had failed to state. It is Lakatos's method mechanised, and he would have recognised it at once.

The second is that everything we currently measure becomes a worse measure, and does so first in the mid-tier where most careers are made. What replaces a degraded proxy, if nothing is done, is *reputation*: a considerably more biased instrument, and one that disadvantages precisely the early-career and peripherally-placed researchers the [Leiden Declaration on Artificial Intelligence and Mathematics](https://leidendeclaration.ai/) is worried about. The bite is worst in the middle of the distribution, and it is worth being precise about why, since a later section of this post argues that the *tasks* machines take over are scattered rather than concentrated in any band. The concentration claimed here is about how work is valued rather than about what is automatable. Near the top, people are assessed on standing; at the bottom there was no research career to disturb; in the middle, a portfolio of solid papers is the currency, and a portfolio is what stops signalling once portfolios are cheap to produce. This is the sharpest internal tension in the optimistic story. [Formal verification](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Proof_assistant) is supposed to let an unknown contributor be taken seriously without personal trust; the volume problem pushes journals and readers toward reputational gating. Both effects are real, they run in opposite directions, and which dominates is a policy choice rather than a technological inevitability.

Priority deserves separating out, because it is the proxy the profession is most attached to and the one that fails most completely. Being first is a claim about a result, and a claim about a result is worth something only once the result has a settled status. If generation outruns verification, priority claims accumulate faster than the verification that would give them content: thousands of announcements nobody has checked, in a world where nobody has much incentive to check them, since checking is unpaid and generating is cheap. The tempting conclusion is that priority collapses. I think that is wrong, and worth resisting, because credit institutions have proved unusually durable under strain. Priority survived the arXiv, it survives routine simultaneous discovery, and it survived Perelman declining both the Fields Medal and the Clay prize without anybody concluding that the concept had stopped working.

The likelier outcome is that priority *migrates*. If being first to a claim stops being scarce, credit attaches to whatever remains scarce: who posed the problem, who verified it, who ran and steered the system, who wrote the account that people actually read. [Mochizuki's case](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inter-universal_Teichm%C3%BCller_theory), a claimed proof of the abc conjecture published in 2021 and still unaccepted, is instructive in exactly that respect rather than as a foretaste of collapse. The priority claim has stood for fourteen years without acquiring the status that would make it worth having, because status was never conferred by the claim but by the community's acceptance of it, and that mechanism is untouched by any of this.

Migration is also the more useful prediction, because it can fail in specifiable ways. It implies that the visible markers of esteem move: that acknowledgements lengthen, that verification and exposition begin appearing in citation practice, that prize citations start naming contributions other than the proof. If in ten years credit still attaches predominantly to first announcement of a solved problem, this passage was wrong.

Separating two senses of the word also reconciles things this series says about priority that otherwise sit badly together. Priority as a *ranking*, the convention that a result belongs to whoever reached it first, is an adaptation to scarcity and can retire with the scarcity, which is why the anxiety about being scooped appears below among the norms worth losing rather than among the losses. Priority as a *provenance record*, the plain question of what was claimed when and by whom, becomes more important rather than less, because it is what keeps accountability assignable once credit floats free. The proposals in [the last post of this series](/blog/what-to-do-about-proof-abundance/) that make priority harder to establish are costly in the second sense, and that is the sense in which the cost should be counted.

[Timothy Gowers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Timothy_Gowers), in [his own response to the Leiden Declaration](https://gowers.wordpress.com/2026/07/26/thoughts-about-the-leiden-declaration/), pushes this further than I would have dared, and supplies the constructive half. If theorems stop being associated with particular mathematicians, he suggests, that may in the end be no more troubling than the fact that stars are not named after astronomers and most are not named at all. What matters is where the credit goes instead, and there he offers a concrete rule. Suppose one person gets a model to one-shot the solution of an important open problem, formalised so that its correctness is not in doubt, while another does the work of digesting that solution and explaining it so that other mathematicians can learn from it. The second should receive the larger share. And the *kind* of credit changes with it: less admiration for talent, insight and speed, more the sort of gratitude we already feel toward someone who writes a textbook that makes a whole area coherent. That is the reweighting this section argues for, stated as a distribution rule rather than as an aspiration, and it is the most usable version of it I have seen.

It also invites the objection that does most damage to this series, and Gowers is the one who raises it. His habit is to ask, of any proposed future role for humans, why an AI could not do that as well. Applied to his own suggestion: what reason is there to think a sufficiently capable model could not hold a short conversation about your background and tastes and then write the ideal textbook for you? If exposition and digestion are themselves automatable, then the whole recommendation above, the lecture's and mine alike, is a holding action rather than a solution. It relocates the human contribution to a place the tools have not reached yet, not to a place they cannot reach. I have no answer to that. The most I can say is that the bottleneck argument does not require digestion to be permanently human, only to be the scarce input now, and that a holding action buying a decade of coherent practice is not nothing. His own fallback is that what would finally be lost is mathematics as a collective endeavour, the pleasure of working something out in company, and that even this could in principle be manufactured by a system deliberately teaching many people the same things, which raises problems of a different order. He is worth reading closely here because he is often taken for a cheerleader and is not one: he notes that when he calls an AI solution remarkable he means only that it surprised him, and reports that having a model one-shot two problems he had thought hard about felt like having the rug pulled from under him, unpleasant in much the way that being scooped by another mathematician is unpleasant.

## The case for optimism

Stripped of hype, the plausible gains are these, in roughly descending order of confidence.

1. *Literature search and cross-field connection.* The most underrated near-term win, and Tao [singles it out](https://mathstodon.xyz/@tao/115385022005130505) for the right reason: it is a task an expert *could* also do, which is what makes the output verifiable. A good deal of duplicated effort in mathematics is a failure of search rather than of ability.
2. *The tedious verified layer.* Explicit constants, case checks, routine estimates, lemma-level formalisation. Tao's [progress report on explicit analytic number theory](https://icerm.brown.edu/video_archive/4649) makes the general point: hand the machine the labour that nobody wanted as a career, and nothing valuable is displaced. His related observation that [autoformalization has begun to empty the queue of unclaimed formalisation tasks](https://mathstodon.xyz/@tao/116789373239346609) suggests this is already happening.
3. *Construction and counterexample search.* This gives fields with parameterised families of explicit objects a genuinely new instrument: sweep the parameter space, verify cheaply at the end.
4. *Population studies.* Clearing the tail of the literature that was neglected for want of attention rather than for want of ability.
5. *Trustless collaboration.* Formalisation removes the personal-trust bottleneck that caps human projects at a handful of people. The formalisation of the polynomial Freiman–Ruzsa conjecture, a thirty-three-page paper decomposed into a blueprint and completed in about three weeks by roughly twenty mostly unacquainted people, is the existence proof.

Beyond that list, Kontorovich makes two arguments I would not have arrived at, and both are stronger than anything in my own optimistic case. The first is about the structure of mathematical responsibility. At present accountability is intensely personal and *vertical*: when you cite a theorem you feel obliged to understand its proof, and the discipline is accordingly organised like a workshop in which every engineer mines their own ore and forges their own parts. A formal statement, by contrast, specifies exactly what is assumed and exactly what is guaranteed, which is what makes a component reusable. If that specification could be trusted, mathematics could become genuinely modular, and responsibility could shift from vertical to horizontal. What that would change is not the speed of any individual proof but the size of the object a research programme can reasonably attempt.

The second is a possible answer to my own gloomiest prediction. Blueprint tooling, which links a natural-language LaTeX document to the corresponding formal code and generates a navigable dependency graph, as in the [prime number theorem project](https://github.com/AlexKontorovich/PrimeNumberTheoremAnd), exists for a mundane engineering reason: it shows collaborators which leaves of the tree still need attention. But it also points toward proof at multiple resolutions, where an argument can be explored at whatever granularity the reader needs. A referee could then verify the logical architecture of a paper and descend into detailed proofs only for the claims that are novel or that smell wrong. That is precisely a triage technology, and triage is the thing I argue below is about to fail. It is worth naming as the most plausible route by which the referee problem gets solved rather than merely endured.

A third argument cuts against my own pessimism below, and I think it is underrated. [Michael Kearns and Aaron Roth](https://www.amazon.science/blog/how-ai-is-changing-the-nature-of-mathematical-research), who used agentic tools to produce a fifty-page paper in three weeks, point out that the lemma their system supplied was one they simply did not know, drawn from an area they were not expert in, and that this lowers the barrier to discovery specifically for researchers *without access to a wide circle of collaborators*. That is worth dwelling on, because collaborator access is precisely what early-career researchers, small departments and peripherally located mathematicians lack. The tool is a substitute for the very advantage that reputation-based gating protects. So there are two opposite equity effects in play, degraded proxies pushing evaluation toward reputation and cheap expertise substituting for network access, and which dominates is an empirical question rather than something either optimists or pessimists get to assume.

Then there is the constructive answer to the Goodhart problem, which comes from [the *Notices* report on the Leiden workshop](https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.24914) rather than from the lecture that names the problem. If a measure is going to be targeted regardless, the community should own the measure. Commelin and his co-authors propose community-built benchmarks precisely on these grounds: they signal to developers what mathematicians actually value, they help mathematicians calibrate what the tools can and cannot do, and benchmarks built around open-ended problems are the natural corrective to sweeping claims resting on narrow task suites. [First Proof](https://1stproof.org/), with its unpublished problems, controlled conditions and anonymous human refereeing, looks like the first serious instance of exactly this. The same article makes the parallel argument for the formal libraries: they need governance, licensing frameworks and contributor norms that protect them from unrestricted commercial use without attribution, and more generally the community needs academically oriented infrastructure that is open source, transparent and under community oversight, so that it does not depend on any single commercial actor. That is the concrete form of the intellectual-autonomy worry, and it is more actionable than any amount of principle.

There is also a specific and cheerful form of the capability argument that deserves separating from the long-tail one. Kontorovich's example is the finite-field Kakeya conjecture, which absorbed years of incremental effort from very strong people and then [fell in 2008 to a short argument](https://terrytao.wordpress.com/2008/03/24/dvirs-proof-of-the-finite-field-kakeya-conjecture/), Zeev Dvir's [polynomial method](https://www.cs.princeton.edu/~zdvir/papers/Dvir09.pdf), elementary enough to present in full on a blog. What had looked deeply hard was in fact reachable by a technique nobody had thought to import. If mathematics contains many such problems, difficult not intrinsically but because no human had the stamina to try the right combination of tools from unrelated fields, then a system that can cheaply explore combinations, with verification at the end, is well matched to exactly that.

It should be said at once that this is the same observation as the harvest account [discussed at the end of this series](/blog/what-to-do-about-proof-abundance/), with the sign reversed. Both hold that a large body of results is reachable by combining what already exists, and that humans left it unreached for want of patience rather than for want of ability. They differ only over the size of that body. Read as an expansion, the reachable results are numerous enough that clearing them constitutes a genuine advance; read as a harvest, they are a finite inventory whose clearance tells us little about what comes after. Nothing currently known settles which, and the same evidence is being offered on both sides. This is not the claim that machines will crack the Riemann hypothesis. It is the more modest and more likely claim that a certain kind of famous problem was never as hard as its reputation.

There is also a reflex, worth resisting, that treats abundance itself as the problem. The transition Tao describes, from an era of proof scarcity to one of proof abundance, is without precedent in the subject, and a good deal of the discipline's apparatus is an adaptation to scarcity rather than a value in its own right: the secrecy about work in progress, the anxiety about being scooped, the convention that a result belongs to whoever reached it first. Norms built to ration a scarce good do not need defending when the good stops being scarce; they need retiring. The genuinely hard question is which of our practices are of that kind and which encode something we would miss, and it is hard because the two are thoroughly mixed together and the profession has never before had occasion to tell them apart.

And finally an honest by-product: even if capability plateaued tomorrow, the discipline would be better for having had to say out loud what it is for. Tao's framing of the present as a crisis in the foundations of *practice*, analogous to the foundational crisis of 1900–1930, turbulent but ending in something explicit and rigorous and trusted, seems to me exactly right, and it is the strongest reason to engage with the debate even from a position of capability scepticism.

## The case for concern

The Leiden threat list is good and I will not restate it. Four additions, where the discussion seems to me thin.

*The binding failure will be referee supply, not fake theorems.* Submission volume is elastic; referee capacity is not, is unpaid, and is already strained. The visible near-term failure is a queueing collapse: longer delays, more desk rejections, more reliance on author reputation, and a quiet rise in the number of published results that nobody has actually checked. This damages the literature even if every AI-assisted paper in it is correct.

Kontorovich makes the sharper version of this argument, and made it first, so let me give it in his form rather than mine. Imagine a system that produces a hundred research papers a day, of which ninety-nine are entirely correct, an extraordinarily favourable assumption. Would you use it? To use it at all you must read all hundred to find the one that is wrong, and verifying a research-level paper is not obviously cheaper than producing one. You would spend every day confirming work that was already correct, while the single flawed paper slips through precisely when its error is subtle. Nor can you extract the value of the ninety-nine without checking them, since you cannot build on a theorem you have not verified, at which point you may as well have done the mathematics yourself. Improving the accuracy to 99.99 per cent changes nothing structural, because the problem is not the error rate but the impossibility of telling which is which without doing the work.

The mechanism underneath that is what matters, and it is the piece my own version was missing. Two qualifications, set out three paragraphs below, weaken it considerably, and the arguments that follow in this series should be read as resting on the weaker form: that machine output degrades whatever triage signals referees do use, not specifically that polish was the load-bearing one. We do not in fact check everything we read; we triage, using signals that correlate with care: the quality of the exposition, whether standard techniques are being used in standard ways, how edge cases are handled, whether the claims are plausible given what we already know. This is why a brilliant paper from an unknown author can be recognised as such. [Kevin Buzzard](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kevin_Buzzard)'s observation, which Kontorovich quotes, is that a model is optimising for plausibility rather than for truth, and will smooth over a gap as readily as fill it, so the correlation between polish and reliability breaks, and with it the filter. When a hundred papers a day issue from a single source, the signals provide no differentiation and everything must be treated with equal suspicion. The reviewing system does not fail because there is more to read. It fails because the cheap heuristic that told us what to read carefully has stopped carrying information. And this is exactly why the reputational gating discussed above is such an unattractive equilibrium: when every other triage signal degrades, reputation is the one that survives, and it is the most biased instrument in the set.

Here are the two qualifications promised above, and both should be said plainly. The first is that referees may not triage on polish at all. Plausibly they triage on venue, on author, and on whether the topic is one they can judge, with the quality of exposition mattering only at the margin. If that is the real filter, then AI degrades a signal that never carried much weight, and the problem reduces to raw volume, which desk rejection handles crudely but handles. The second is that the machine learning analogy may not transfer. Submission volume there is elastic because conference deadlines and count-based hiring reward volume directly, whereas a mathematician who submits weak work under their own name pays a reputational cost in a small community with a long memory. Neither point makes mathematics safe. Both mean the claim that it is two years behind on the same curve is a conjecture about institutional similarity rather than an observation.

Kearns and Roth add the reframing that makes the stakes clear. Peer review's primary function, they argue, is not verifying correctness but *allocating a scarce resource*, namely the attention of the research community. There is already far more work than anyone can follow; publication exists to mark out which directions are worth developing. On that reading, what AI threatens is not a quality-control check but the discipline's attention-allocation mechanism, which is the thing that makes knowledge cumulative rather than merely large. Their formulation of the danger is the sharpest I have seen and arrives at my central claim from the opposite direction: without a community-wide rethinking of review, AI may arrest progress at the level of the community even while accelerating it at the level of the individual researcher.

Mathematics has one considerable advantage here, which is that it can watch the experiment run next door. Machine learning conferences already handle tens of thousands of submissions per venue, and are [reporting](https://www.theatlantic.com/science/2026/01/ai-slop-science-publishing/685704/) submission counts growing by multiples, AI-polished but empty papers travelling surprisingly far through review before anyone notices, a market in generated papers and a countermarket in generated-paper detection: the familiar shape of a spam arms race, with the integrity of the literature as the stake. Mathematics is perhaps two years behind on this curve and has the rare luxury of seeing the failure mode before living it. The response being tried there is worth watching too. Automated correctness checking as a first-pass filter, unit testing for mathematics, is [already deployed experimentally at ICML](https://blog.icml.cc/2026/01/14/icml-experimental-program-using-googles-paper-assistant-tool-pat/), with the explicit aim of freeing human referees from checking technical correctness so they can spend their attention on whether a result matters. That is the same manoeuvre as blueprint triage, arriving from software rather than from formalisation, and the convergence of two independent communities on it is mild evidence that it is the right one.

There is now a third convergence, and it is the most concrete answer to the triage problem that anyone has proposed. [Tobias Osborne](https://tjoresearchnotes.wordpress.com/2026/08/02/reaping-without-sowing/)'s observation about the pipeline he built to mine open problems from the literature and set agents on the most promising is that the identical machinery, pointed at verification rather than production, becomes the strongest reviewing instrument the discipline has ever had: fleets of hostile referees, provenance audits on every quotation and citation, and sweeps for whether a claimed result is actually open, all for a few hundred dollars a night. His experiment supplies the existence proof, since among his eight attacker agents one produced a rigorous counterexample to the very hypothesis it had been assigned to prove, and another surfaced a problem that had been answered a few lines below where it was posed. Symmetry is what makes this plausible. The same asymmetry that makes generation cheap makes adversarial checking cheap, and the second is easier than the first, because refuting requires only one witness. If the reviewing system is rescued, this is the likeliest mechanism, and it requires no institutional permission from anybody.

Not everyone takes preserving the review system to be the goal. The Declaration asks mathematicians to insist that results continue to appear in peer-reviewed venues, and Gowers declines to support that recommendation: he says that if the current publication system became unsustainable under a flood of AI-generated and AI-assisted content, he would count that a benefit. He is not arguing for a free-for-all, since his central worry is precisely that insufficiently organised mathematics lets the tradition die. His point is that whatever preserves the tradition is likely to be more inventive than clinging to the journal. I think he is right about journals and understates the function they presently perform, which is the attention allocation described above. The question is not whether to keep the institution but whether anything is being built to take over its job.

*Displacement is likelier to be scattered than banded.* The natural expectation, borrowed from labour markets where automation took routine work first, is that the middle of the distribution goes first: not the conceptual breakthroughs and not the routine calculations, but the large stock of hard-but-not-deep problems on which most careers are actually built. That expectation is worth stating because most people hold it, and it rests on an assumption the evidence here does not support. Banding requires machine capability to line up with the human difficulty ordering, and it does not appear to. The [unit-distance disproof](https://openai.com/index/model-disproves-discrete-geometry-conjecture/), in which a model beat the square grid by constructing point configurations nobody had gone looking for, was a hard problem that fell to patience in a search space, while the First Proof referees and the practitioner reports describe systems failing on fiddly things no expert would call deep. If capability is orthogonal to difficulty rather than correlated with it, displacement lands in scattered patches determined by the *shape* of a problem rather than by its rank, and no band is protected.

The practical consequence inverts the usual advice, which is why it matters. You cannot make yourself safe by working on harder problems. Whether a line of work is exposed depends on whether it terminates in something checkable, how long its inference chains are, and whether it can be reached by search, and none of those track difficulty.

That is a claim about which *tasks* are exposed, though, and the career question is separate. There the concentration argument survives intact, because it never needed capability to track difficulty. It needs only that work be rewarded differently at different points in the distribution. Near the top, people are hired and promoted for who they are, and reputational standing insulates them from any particular result becoming cheap to produce. At the bottom there was no career to displace. In the middle, output is the currency: a tenure case, a grant renewal or a first permanent post is built from a portfolio of solid papers, and a portfolio is precisely what becomes hard to distinguish from a generated one. So the *value* of mid-tier work can fall even where the tasks involved are not the ones machines happen to be good at, and it can fall for reasons unconnected to whether any individual paper was assisted.

Two things follow, and they hold under either reading of the capability question. The effect concentrates where the training pipeline runs, since doctoral and early-career work is mid-tier work almost by definition, which is what makes this more than a distributional curiosity. And the remedy is not technical. If the middle is squeezed because volume stopped signalling anything, what is needed is a different signal rather than a different tool, which returns the problem to the institutions.

*Deskilling we cannot detect until it is too late.* The calculator analogy understates the problem, because arithmetic is testable. The skill at risk here (knowing which of five vague analogies is worth a month) is tested only by outcomes on a decade timescale, by which point the cohort is trained. Tao's honest concession, in his lecture on students' cognitive diets, is that a real and unequally distributed cost is likely, falling on those without the discipline or the environment to resist the easy path.

*The stock of expertise can depreciate faster than the literature grows.* This is Gowers's worry rather than mine, and it goes deeper than the deskilling argument because it concerns the community rather than the individual. Consider a pandemic that killed every mathematician and nobody else. The literature would survive intact, and nobody would have the faintest idea what to do with it; reviving the tradition would take decades. Now consider the mild version, in which AI exists and people are simply no longer motivated to spend the years it takes to reach the expertise a research mathematician has today. After a decade or two the literature has expanded enormously and there is no corresponding community of people who share an understanding of any part of it. Almost all of mathematics would then resemble the areas we have already half forgotten, alive in papers written decades ago that nobody now reads. What makes this more than a restatement of the throughput argument is that the relevant knowledge lives in heads rather than in the literature, and cannot be reconstructed from the literature by anyone who does not already have it. A clogged pipeline can be unclogged. A lost tradition cannot.

*The politics is inseparable, and the community is not united on it.* The Declaration goes well beyond research hygiene. It calls for regulation of the industry, for scrutiny of military and surveillance applications, for environmental accounting, for opt-out from training data, and for withdrawal from work one judges harmful. Some will regard that as the necessary context, and others will regard the bundling of contested political claims with research-practice recommendations as a reason to hold back, or will note the awkwardness of asking mathematicians to weigh their tool suppliers' values when the discipline's own funding has never been ethically clean. The most articulate declining voice, though, gives a quite different reason, and it is better to report it than to guess. Gowers took part in the Leiden workshop and has not signed the Declaration that came out of it, [explaining](https://gowers.wordpress.com/2026/07/26/thoughts-about-the-leiden-declaration/) that his reservation is not concrete disagreement but that the document makes confident assertions and recommendations in several places where he feels real uncertainty, and that he prefers to set out his views and their qualifications at length instead. He is emphatic that he is glad the Declaration exists and that it has done good in focusing attention. That is an epistemic objection rather than a political one, and it is the objection a scientist naturally makes to a manifesto: the form demands more confidence than the evidence currently supports. Tao's position is worth registering alongside it as the considered alternative on the political question: [engage rather than boycott](https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.26524), on the grounds that incentives run both ways, coupled with full disclosure of his own entanglements with the industry. Both stances are defensible; the disagreement is real rather than a misunderstanding.

## What a proof-centred diagnosis leaves out

Everything above treats proof as the object, and the cheapening of proof generation as the event. That framing is inherited from the sources, all of which are about proof, and it may not survive the next few years.

Conjecture is the conspicuous omission. The pipeline the ICM lecture describes begins with open problems already in hand, as though the supply of good questions were exogenous and inexhaustible. It is neither. Deciding what to try to prove is a large part of what research mathematicians do, and it is the part least visible in the literature, since papers report answers rather than the search for questions worth asking. If systems become good at generating conjectures that are simultaneously plausible, non-trivial and interesting, three things follow, none of which are in the analysis above.

The first is that the bottleneck moves again. Digestion is scarce because generation is cheap; if question-generation also becomes cheap, the scarce input becomes judgment about which of a thousand plausible conjectures deserves a year, and that is a capacity almost nobody trains explicitly and no institution measures.

The second is that the evidence would have to look quite different from anything currently collected. Every benchmark discussed here scores answers against known solutions, which is possible only for questions somebody has already asked. There is no equivalent of First Proof for conjecture, and it is not obvious what one would look like, since success is a judgment rendered years later by a community rather than a fact checkable on the day.

The third is that both the optimistic and the pessimistic readings change character. The optimistic version is a genuine expansion: machines proposing questions humans would not have thought to ask, in areas nobody was looking at, which is the strongest form of the hidden-low-hanging-fruit argument. The pessimistic version is that the discipline acquires an inexhaustible supply of technically interesting questions that no human chose, and its agenda stops being set by anything recognisable as taste.

Current evidence is thin in both directions. The [Erdős problems](https://www.erdosproblems.com) work is question-answering against an existing list, and DeepMind's own [case study on that database](https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.22401) stresses that judgments of novelty, relevance and framing remained human. Nothing yet suggests these systems are good at asking. But nothing in the sources here models what follows if they become so, and a reader in three years should treat this as the most likely point at which this series dates.

There is one serious argument that the obstacle is structural rather than temporary, and it deserves stating because it is the best-developed thing available on the question. In [*LLMs can't jump*](https://www.tomzahavy.com/files/llms-cant-jump.pdf), a position paper dated 27 January 2026 and [deposited in the PhilSci-Archive](https://philsci-archive.pitt.edu/28024/), Tom Zahavy, a researcher at DeepMind, takes Einstein's formulation of general relativity as a case study and argues that generative systems have mastered induction, are rapidly acquiring deduction, and possess no mechanism at all for the third thing: the generation of novel explanatory hypotheses. He concedes a great deal along the way, including that a modern model initialised with Einstein's 1915 assumptions could plausibly derive the field equations, since that is a verifiable logical task. What it could not do, on his account, is arrive at the assumptions.

Three qualifications belong with it, because the paper is circulating in a considerably stronger form than it was written in. It is a single-author position paper carrying a DeepMind copyright line rather than an institutional position, and its author's note describes it as a personal inquiry and disclaims expertise in physics. It does not say machines can never do this: it says current architectures cannot, and proposes a route, namely world models that let an agent intervene in a simulation rather than merely predict it. And its closing paragraph limits the *proposal* to the physical sciences, where the object of study is external material reality. That restriction is easy to get backwards, so it is worth stating precisely. He holds the necessity of the abductive jump to be universal, applying to mathematics as much as to physics. What he says does not carry across is the remedy. For physics the substrate of sensory experience is the world; for mathematics he suggests it is the abstract landscape of formal systems, with the relevant experience perhaps grounded in high-dimensional structure, or in goals such as generality and minimality. The diagnosis is offered as general. Only the proposed cure is specific to physics.

What does transfer is the argument about error signals, and it is sharp. When Einstein began, Newtonian gravity faced no empirical crisis. The equivalence of inertial and gravitational mass had been confirmed to about a part in a billion, and the one anomaly, the precession of Mercury's perihelion, was widely attributed to an undiscovered planet rather than to any defect in the theory. A system optimising against observation would have found the loss already near zero and no gradient pointing anywhere. Zahavy's illustration of the resulting failure mode is the useful part: such a system would sooner posit the planet Vulcan than adopt non-Euclidean geometry, because restructuring makes things more complicated before it makes them simpler. Logical simplicity, as he puts it, is often a retrospective property.

That translates into mathematics directly, and it has a testable consequence. It predicts a *direction* of bias in machine-assisted work: toward strengthening existing statements, adding hypotheses and extending results to one more class, and away from reframings that increase complexity in the short run. Given the argument earlier in this post that digestion is where statements acquire their content, this is the same worry arriving from the optimisation side, and the ratio of incremental strengthenings to genuine reframings within a subfield is a countable thing.

The deeper point is that his diagnosis and the one in [the first post of this series](/blog/generation-is-hard-checking-is-cheap/) are a single claim seen from two directions. Zahavy says machines work where there is an error signal to descend. This series says they are absorbed where verification is cheap. Both describe the presence of an objective that already exists to be optimised against, and the abductive case is precisely the one in which the objective must be invented before anything can be optimised at all. That is worth stating, because it means the gap he identifies is predicted by the framework used here rather than lying outside it, and because it accounts for the evidence assembled earlier. The unit-distance disproof had an objective, which was to beat the square grid. AlphaEvolve, as he notes, performs well within a fixed framework because it has a gradient to follow. The fractal constructions [predicted in a later post](/blog/where-the-argument-bends/) to fall first are exactly those with a checkable target at the end.

Applied to mathematics rather than physics, his own framework decomposes the conjecture question more usefully than I managed above. He distinguishes inventing variations of a problem within a fixed set of rules, which is what test-time reinforcement learning does and what carried AlphaProof to Olympiad standard, from inventing the rules themselves. Symbolic variation inside a fixed axiom system is demonstrated capability. Producing the axioms is the gap. In our terms the question is therefore not whether machines can generate conjectures, since generating them within an existing framework is a search problem with a track record. It is whether they can generate the *definitions* that make a new class of conjecture statable, which is exactly where Lakatos located the content of mathematics and exactly what digestion produces.

That reframing raises the question his closing paragraph leaves open, and the answer available is more encouraging than his physical case. If the jump requires grounding in something prior to symbols, what plays that role for a mathematician? The honest answer is examples: computing special cases, drawing pictures, perturbing a construction to see what breaks, acquiring a feel for how a class of objects behaves before any theorem about it exists. That is the mathematician's version of thinking by doing, and it is not obviously out of reach. It is closer to Tao's population studies, and to the parameter sweeps described in [a later post of this series](/blog/where-the-argument-bends/), than to anything requiring a body. On his own account the obstacle in physics is that a language model cannot get at the physical referents. Mathematics may be the discipline in which the referents are already symbolic, which is to say already within reach. If the argument has a weak point for our purposes, it is here.

This is also the point at which the old metaphysical question stops being decorative and starts making predictions. Zahavy calls the mathematical substrate the abstract landscape of formal systems, which is Platonist vocabulary, and the suggestion above that a mathematician's sense experience consists of examples only makes sense if there is a terrain to poke at. Take the two readings seriously and they diverge on exactly the question at issue. If the abductive jump is *perception* of structure that is already there, then patient, broad, systematic search over a real landscape is a way of getting to it, and machines have the advantage rather than the deficit, since patience and breadth are what they have. If the jump is instead *legislation*, the creation of a concept that did not exist before anyone framed it, then search cannot reach it, because until the concept exists there is nothing there to search. The invented and discovered readings thus map fairly directly onto the two accounts weighed at [the end of this series](/blog/what-to-do-about-proof-abundance/), which is not a correspondence I would have expected to find, and it means the metaphysics has a testable shadow even though the metaphysics itself does not.

The paper also contains, without dwelling on it, a good instance of the opposite case. Einstein and Grossmann had the right object in hand in 1913: they had identified the Riemann curvature tensor and contracted it to something close to what we now call the Einstein tensor. They abandoned it because Grossmann concluded it failed to reduce to Newtonian gravity in the weak static limit, and that conclusion was mistaken. The error lay not in the geometry but in an auxiliary assumption about static fields, and it cost two years. Zahavy notes that current systems could plausibly have caught precisely this, by systematically varying which constraints are imposed and seeing which subsets survive. That is the junk-value problem of [the first post](/blog/generation-is-hard-checking-is-cheap/) in historical dress, and it is a useful reminder that the same analysis identifying what these systems cannot do also identifies, quite exactly, what they could have saved.

One further concession cuts in mathematics' favour and deserves recording, since it inverts the comfort a mathematician might take from the paper's title. Even granted the deductive capacity, he observes, a problem of intent remains: Einstein was not trying to prove a theorem but to build a predictive model of reality, with no agreed target and no verification available for years. Formal theorem proving comes with the goal supplied. On his own reasoning, then, mathematics should fall to these systems earlier and more completely than physics, precisely because it hands them something to aim at.

Two things in the paper I would not take along with the argument. The division of inference into induction, deduction and abduction is Peirce's, it is contested, and the framework already in use here predicts the same phenomenon without it. And the paper is not evidence in the sense the benchmarks are. It is one historical case study, selected because it is the strongest instance of its own thesis, containing no counterfactual and nothing measured.

## The end of heroism

There is a mechanism behind the profession's distress that the institutional analysis above does not reach, and the [most articulate statement of it](https://www.noahpinion.blog/p/the-end-of-the-age-of-heroes) comes from an economist rather than a mathematician. Noah Smith's argument is that mathematical heroism was never simply a matter of some people being cleverer. It was a structural consequence of how knowledge had to travel.

Society can hold ideas far more complex than any individual can grasp, but it transmits them through individuals. A theorem has to be expressed so that one student can learn it and one colleague can be taught it, which means that every idea entering the collective had ultimately to be comprehensible to a single human being, and therefore to have been conceived by one. The people who could conceive of the barely conceivable were the heroes, and mathematics produced more of them than most fields because its ideas came in units that a single mind could, with difficulty, hold whole. On this account a machine that is not limited by what one brain can apprehend does not merely compete with such people. It removes the constraint that made them necessary.

That is a genuinely different mechanism from the ones above, and it deserves a name of its own because it explains something the incentive analysis does not: why the reaction has been grief rather than calculation. It also connects to the oldest thread in this debate. The demand that a proof be surveyable, which Tymoczko pressed against the four colour theorem and drew from Wittgenstein, is exactly the demand that mathematics keep its ideas in single-human units. The heroic age and the surveyability requirement are the same fact seen from two sides.

Smith's own qualification undercuts him somewhat, and he puts it in a footnote: society already handles ideas larger than any individual by breaking them into pieces that individuals can hold. That is modularity, which is the same property discussed earlier as the promise of formal specification, and it means the single-human bottleneck was never quite as absolute as the argument requires. The classification of finite simple groups was not conceived by one person and nobody thinks it therefore failed to enter mathematics. What is true is narrower and still substantial: for a certain class of contributions, the ones we have most conspicuously honoured, the constraint was real, and it is going.

His larger framing is that AI is best understood not as a replacement for individual workers but as a new collective intelligence, with human researchers occupying the position of [edge compute](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Edge_computing): going out into the world, mixing what they find with what they were taught, and reporting back. The picture is unflattering and not obviously wrong. It also has a corollary this series should sit with, which is that on this reading every mathematician writing a paper today is contributing to the training corpus whether or not they intend to, and that the commons argument made earlier is a description of the ordinary condition of research rather than of a novel injustice.

## What it costs the people doing it

Everything above is institutional, and an account that stays institutional will misdescribe what is actually happening in the profession this summer, which is grief.

The reaction to the [August results](https://openai.com/index/ten-advances-in-mathematics/), ten problems open for decades announced as settled with machine-checkable certificates attached, included a widely read [post by a mathematician describing a spiritual crisis](https://kirwinhampshire.substack.com/p/the-dark-night-of-mathematics), written with unusual honesty and offering, in its author's own words, no prescriptions and no coherent call to action. It is worth reading, and it is worth reading as testimony rather than as argument, since it does not claim to be one. A graduate student has [called for a confrontational response](https://tasmin.substack.com/p/mathematicians-need-to-act), urging mathematicians to refuse collaboration with the companies and to defend existing practice directly, which is a position this post's earlier discussion of engagement versus boycott treated too tidily as a strategic choice. Even mathematicians with no sympathy for either stance have [expressed alarm](https://x.com/wtgowers/status/2052830957879599513) at the pace.

The clearest statement of what is at stake came from Jacob Tsimerman, who [wrote](https://x.com/Jacob_Tsimerman/status/2051116022585770170) that he does mathematics not only to find truth but because he enjoys it and is good at it, because he finds it beautiful, and because he likes the challenge: the process, the resolving of confusions, the finding of strategies, the grappling. His conclusion is the part worth quoting to any administrator: for many people the primary enjoyment of mathematics comes through problem solving in one of its forms, and if that disappears, this is not a trivial issue, and many of them may not want to do it any more. He has since taken a position in industry, which is itself a datum about where the profession's incentives now point.

Three things follow, none of them consoling.

The first is that motivation is a real input and the analysis above treats it as a constant. Every institutional remedy proposed in [the last post of this series](/blog/what-to-do-about-proof-abundance/), from digestion papers to nominate-and-examine, assumes people who want to keep doing this. If the thing they wanted to do was solve problems, and solving is what became cheap, then the supply of willing labour is itself endogenous to the change, and no reweighting of prestige fixes that. The discipline could find itself with well-designed incentives and nobody who wants them.

The second is that the consolations on offer are weaker than they look, though not empty. The common analogy is chess: a game the machines have owned since 1997 and which is more popular now than it has ever been, with more players, more money and more attention than in the era when a human world champion was the strongest player alive. That is a genuine counterexample to the despair, and it should be taken seriously. But chess is a game with an audience, and the analogy holds only to the degree that mathematics is one too. The parts of the subject with spectators, competitions, exposition, the pleasure of watching someone brilliant, may well flourish exactly as chess did. The parts whose value lay in being useful to somebody are in a different position.

The third is that mathematicians are experiencing at global scale something that has happened locally and quietly to a great many people. The skills that conferred standing in a community, diagnosing an engine by ear, identifying birds by song, knowing the roads, keeping a beautiful hand, have been made unnecessary one after another, and the people who held them received no essays. This does not diminish the loss. It does mean the profession is joining a large and mostly unconsoled company, and that a response framed as a special claim on the world's sympathy will not travel well.

For departments, this is not merely a philosophical matter but a duty of care, and it should be treated with the seriousness that phrase implies. People whose working lives have been organised around a particular kind of achievement are being told, in public and at speed, that the achievement is becoming cheap. That is a recognisable occupational shock with recognisable consequences, and the fact that it is happening to unusually clever people does not make it less so. Institutions that would notice this in any other setting should notice it here.

## What comes apart, and what does not

The goals of mathematics were joint products of one scarce input, and cheap generation decouples a single node of that network while leaving the others where they were. What follows is that the scarce factor moves to verification and digestion, that most of what the profession measures stops measuring anything, and that the first thing to fail is not the truth of the literature but the cheap heuristic by which a reader decides what to read carefully. A reviewing crisis arrives well before a truth crisis, and it arrives even if every AI-assisted paper is correct.

Two things survive the argument, and they are worth separating from the alarm. Priority is likely to migrate rather than collapse, since credit institutions have proved durable under strain and status was never conferred by a claim but by the community's acceptance of it; what changes is where esteem attaches, which is a prediction that can be checked in ten years' worth of acknowledgements and prize citations. And the squeeze on the middle of the distribution is a claim about how work is valued rather than about which tasks machines happen to be good at. Those two come apart, and the remedy for the first is a different signal rather than a better tool.

Two things also remain outside the reach of the argument. Nothing above models what happens if machines become good at asking questions rather than answering them, which is the point at which this diagnosis would need rewriting rather than extending. And no account of incentives explains the reaction the profession is actually having, which is grief at the removal of a constraint that made a certain kind of achievement necessary, and which departments would be well advised to treat as the occupational shock it is.
