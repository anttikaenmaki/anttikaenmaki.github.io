I saw the film last week, and as a film I enjoyed it a great deal, even though, for all the intricacy of its telling, what it has to say is fairly straightforward: a story about post-traumatic stress, about change, about the consequences of one's actions and coming to terms with the past. In other words, it is what in Finnish is wryly called *vanha mies muistelee*, an old man looking back on his life. The scene that stayed with me comes near the end: [Odysseus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Odysseus), disguised as a beggar, sits with his wife and tells her what he has lived through. It is extremely powerful. It is worth knowing what the same scene is in the poem: [there the disguised Odysseus tells Penelope an invented tale](https://www.poetryintranslation.com/PITBR/Greek/Odyssey19.php) of having once hosted her husband, she weeps at a story about herself told by the man she is weeping for, and whether she already suspects him is a question scholars have argued for generations. The film offers a confession where the poem offers a riddle. That difference, it turns out, is what this whole essay is about.

I do not normally write about anything other than mathematics; this blog exists mainly to explain my own research, and as such, this is an exercise for me. But the text below is written the way I write mathematics: state the claim precisely, check it against the sources, and say plainly which steps are established and which are interpretation. Several of the sharpest readings quoted here come from pseudonymous accounts in an online argument rather than from classicists. I have weighted them by the quality of the reading rather than the credential behind it, and where a claim is a matter of fact rather than interpretation, I have gone to the texts. Where I describe what happens on screen without a citation, the source is the film itself.

There are three things a film can do with each theme of the story beneath it. It can honour the theme, it can miss it, or it can turn it to its own purposes. The most revealing thing about the argument now surrounding [Christopher Nolan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Christopher_Nolan)'s [*The Odyssey*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Odyssey_%282026_film%29) is that its sharpest critics do not reach for the second option. The ones who do, like [Stelios Panagiotou](https://x.com/Panagiotou90St), who [calls the film](https://x.com/Panagiotou90St/status/2078616829191397849) a "gross misunderstanding" of the epic's philosophy, are the least convincing for exactly that reason, because the film is too accurate to be a misreading. It finds one of the poem's two ethical centres, [*xenia*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Xenia_%28Greek%29), the sacred bond between host and guest, and builds everything on it; the other, [*nostos*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nostos), the homecoming, it will spend the film dismantling. It knows the poem's architecture, its singers, its recognition scenes. It does not stumble over [Homer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Homer). On the themes that matter most, it performs the third option, and performs it deliberately. What makes the case interesting is that naming the turn does not settle the argument about it, and that the turn only comes into focus when the film is set beside the wider tradition of the epic rather than beside the [*Odyssey*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Odyssey) alone. There the critics turn out to be right about where the film ends up and only half-right about how it gets there: what they call a modern imposition is, in its materials, older and more canonical than they think, though its age does not neutralise what they are reacting to.

## The real Trojan horse: switching Homer for Virgil

Start with the mechanism, which [Kyrios Mykonos](https://x.com/kyriosmykonoss) [sees most clearly](https://x.com/kyriosmykonoss/status/2078567152483971320). The film's founding motif is the [Trojan Horse](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trojan_Horse) as Odysseus's original sin, an offering of peace that broke [Zeus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zeus)'s law of hospitality and set an entire civilisation on the road to collapse. But the full story of the horse, and the character of [Sinon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sinon), whom Nolan [imports](https://variety.com/2026/film/news/the-odyssey-changes-christopher-nolan-adapts-homer-1236814443/), is not Homer. Sinon appears in neither the [*Iliad*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Iliad) nor the *Odyssey*. He belongs to [Virgil](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Virgil)'s [*Aeneid*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aeneid), [where the fall of Troy is told in full](https://www.poetryintranslation.com/PITBR/Latin/VirgilAeneidII.php). In the *Odyssey* the horse is mentioned only in passing. Its full account is Virgil's, and in Virgil the Greeks are the aggressors and the Trojan is the victim.

From this follows the best single insight in the whole debate. When Nolan builds his film's foundation on the horse, he makes us watch the Greek forebears, for the length of the film, through Virgil's eyes rather than Homer's, not as conquerors rewarded for their cunning but as guilty parties, aggressors, breakers of a sacred trust, though Homer himself, as we will see, once turned that gaze on his own hero. This, as Kyrios puts it, is the real Trojan horse of the movie. It is not a modernisation of Homer. It is a change of source. And it explains something the philological complaints, taken one at a time, cannot. Panagiotou [is right](https://x.com/Panagiotou90St/status/2078616829191397849) that the film drops Odysseus's meeting with the souls of his mother [Anticleia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anticlea) and of [Achilles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Achilles), which is [one of the poem's most beautiful and serious scenes](https://www.poetryintranslation.com/PITBR/Greek/Odyssey11.php), yet he treats it as a random omission. It is not. The [nekyia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nekyia) loses them precisely to make room for the invented Sinon, whose death Odysseus is made to have engineered by concealing from him the horse's true purpose. Nor does Sinon merely arrive. He [takes over the narrative role](https://foreignpolicy.com/2026/07/23/odyssey-civilization-collapse-history/) of [Elpenor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Elpenor), the companion who dies unburied in Homer and whose ghost is the first to reach Odysseus among the dead, pleading for a grave. A Virgilian character now stands where a Homeric one stood, which is the whole switch performed in a single substitution. The change is not carelessness. It is the switch, working at the level of structure.

## An ending in Dante's shape

The ending switches sources again. Nolan's Odysseus [abdicates the throne to Telemachus and sails west with Penelope](https://comicbook.com/movies/feature/the-odyssey-ending-explained-why-christopher-nolans-film-differs-from-homers-epic/) into an "unknown west" as a self-imposed exile, penance for the blood on his hands and for breaking Zeus's law. The film makes that ending structurally necessary rather than merely appended. Its suitors weaponise Zeus's law, invoking the claim of the guest to consume [Penelope](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Penelope)'s household, so that killing them is not the law's vindication but one more breach of it, and exile becomes the penalty Odysseus accepts for a violation he cannot avoid committing. [In Homer the same slaughter is justice](https://www.poetryintranslation.com/PITBR/Greek/Odyssey22.php), earned precisely because the suitors abused hospitality, and [Athena](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Athena) halts the reprisal so the king can keep his hall. The identical act is a restoration in one telling and a final sin in the other. Kyrios reads the result correctly as a turn inward, a man whose punishment comes not from the gods but from himself. Its nearest literary ancestor, though, is not the modern therapy culture the traditionalists reach for. It is [Dante](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dante_Alighieri).

In [canto XXVI of the *Inferno*](https://digitaldante.columbia.edu/dante/divine-comedy/inferno/inferno-26/), Ulysses is damned among the false counsellors, condemned in part for the deceit of the horse, and his last act is to sail past the [Pillars of Hercules](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pillars_of_Hercules) into the forbidden western ocean to follow, in his own words, virtue and knowledge, until a whirlwind takes his ship. Nolan's ending is that same shape without the drowning: guilt over the horse, a westward voyage past the edge of the known world, a hero who leaves the world behind. Whether Nolan read the canto or reinvented it hardly matters; no influence is claimed here, only that the shape he arrived at is the shape Dante built, and that it is not Homer's. The two central claims of this essay therefore rest on different footings, and I mark them accordingly: the Virgilian switch is demonstrable on the page, since Sinon exists nowhere else, while the Dantean ending is a kinship of shape. The guilt-structure itself, one transgression that lets ruin into the world, is closer to [Milton](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Milton), where [a single forbidden act brings death into the world](https://milton.host.dartmouth.edu/reading_room/pl/book_1/text.shtml). So the critics' instinct about a Christian lens is sound. The critic [Titus Techera](https://www.civitasinstitute.org/fellows/titus-techera) [traces the specifics](https://www.civitasoutlook.com/research/nolans-odyssey-and-the-search-for-wisdom). The film reframes the Greeks as murderous pirates and the war itself as plunder, reasoning much as [Augustine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Augustine_of_Hippo) did in [The City of God](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_City_of_God), that without justice a kingdom is only robbery on a grand scale, and it rewrites xenia itself, from Homer's Zeus-enforced reciprocal duty into something close to the [Golden Rule](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Golden_Rule) of [Matthew's Gospel](https://www.ccel.org/ccel/bible/kjv.Matt.7.html), a universal and unconditional ethic. Techera even likens Nolan's act of reinterpretation to St Paul telling the Athenians that the [unknown god](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Areopagus_sermon) they already worshipped was Christ. [Johnathan Bi](https://x.com/johnathanbi) [reaches the Golden Rule from the opposite direction](https://x.com/johnathanbi/status/2079613102975279459), reading the change not as a Christian inheritance but as the loss of the asymmetry on which xenia rests, since the Golden Rule presumes a moral symmetry that xenia never did. What is wrong is coding it as rootless modern liberalism. This is a reading of Homer whose lineage is patristic before it is medieval, running from Augustine through Dante to Milton, and on into the modern, where [Yeats](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/W._B._Yeats) compressed the same logic into the fourteen lines of ["Leda and the Swan"](https://www.poetryfoundation.org/poems/43292/leda-and-the-swan), in which a god's violation of a girl leads without a pause to the walls of Troy in flames and [Agamemnon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Agamemnon) murdered. It is part of the very West that the particularists, who hold these stories to be the inheritance of a particular people rather than common property, say they are defending.

The premise underneath it, though, is Homer's own. That any stranger might be a god in disguise is imported from nowhere. [In Book 17](https://www.poetryintranslation.com/PITBR/Greek/Odyssey17.php) a suitor rebukes [Antinous](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Antinous_of_Ithaca) for striking the beggar on exactly that ground, since the gods roam the cities disguised as strangers from afar, watching who keeps the law and who does not. What is modern in Nolan is not the premise but what he does with it, unhooking a reciprocal and ranked obligation and making it unconditional. Nor does the destination require Christianity. A Greek line running through [Socrates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Socrates)' refusal to return wrong for wrong, [that one must never repay injustice with injustice or harm anyone whatever they have done](https://www.perseus.tufts.edu/hopper/text?doc=Perseus%3Atext%3A1999.01.0170%3Atext%3DCrito%3Asection%3D49c), arrives near the same place, and Israelite tradition was reaching kindred formulations in the same broad era, [forbidding vengeance and commanding that the stranger be loved as one born in the land](https://www.ccel.org/ccel/bible/kjv.Lev.19.html). The convergence is old and multiple rather than a borrowing.

The parallel can be pushed too far, and pushing it too far is itself a way of taking Nolan's side, so two qualifications are owed. Dante's Ulysses sails out of [hubris](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hubris) and forbidden curiosity, a trespass beyond mortal limits, not out of penance. Nolan's guilt is secularised and historicised, tied to the [Late Bronze Age collapse](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Late_Bronze_Age_collapse) and the [Sea Peoples](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sea_Peoples) rather than to sin against a divine order, and it is psychologised into something close to a veteran's self-disqualification. The silhouette is medieval; what fills it is thoroughly modern. What reaches a 2026 audience is contemporary in force even where it is antique in outline, and that is worth conceding plainly, because it is part of what the film's hostile viewers are responding to. The other qualification is that the tradition of Dante and Milton is one deep line of Western reading, not the dominant one. The restorative Homer, the poet of [nostos](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nostos), of homecoming as the thing worth everything, is at least as central to how the West has read the poem, and it is the Homer that [Herod Priovolos](https://x.com/herodpriovolos) [defends](https://x.com/herodpriovolos/status/2078593853972717568). To present the guilt tradition as the true Western inheritance would make the plain, homecoming *Odyssey* more marginal than it is.

## The hero who sails west

Set those qualifications beside the film and a striking thing still remains. Nolan's closing image, the wounded or displaced hero who sails over the water past the world's edge and leaves behind only a story, is one of the deepest currents in the epic imagination, and not only the Western one. [Aeneas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aeneas) flees a burning [Troy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Troy) westward to found a new city. Dante's Ulysses and [Tennyson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alfred,_Lord_Tennyson)'s [Ulysses](https://www.poetryfoundation.org/poems/45392/ulysses) both sail west, and Tennyson drew openly on Homer and Dante at once. [Arthur](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/King_Arthur), mortally wounded, is carried by boat to [Avalon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Avalon) as the once and future king, promised to return. And, the point that lands hardest for a Finnish reader, the [*Kalevala*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kalevala) ends exactly here. When the [lingonberry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lingonberry)-born son of the virgin [Marjatta](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Marjatta) is baptised king of [Karelia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Karelia) and the old pagan world gives way, [Väinämöinen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Väinämöinen), the great singer-sage whose weapon is the word, recognises that his age is over, [sings himself a boat of copper and sails away between earth and sky](https://sacred-texts.com/neu/kveng/kvrune50.htm), leaving his [kantele](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kantele) and his songs to the people and promising to return if ever he is needed. A hero unfit for the new order departs over the water, leaving only song. It is Nolan's Odysseus almost to the line.

Almost, and the gap matters. Väinämöinen yields to a higher, incoming order; his departure is the old making way for the new. Nolan's Odysseus yields to no higher order at all. He punishes himself in a world where the hierarchical and divine order has already collapsed. As an elegiac image the parallel is exact; as a full analogy of motive it is not. The closer match for motive is [Tolkien](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/J._R._R._Tolkien), himself a philologist, whose [Frodo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Frodo_Baggins) saves the Shire but cannot be healed in it, too deeply hurt by the Ring and its loss, and [sails from the Grey Havens over the Sea](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Return_of_the_King). That is Nolan's Odysseus precisely, the saviour for whom homecoming is no longer possible. So the three fit at different depths. Dante supplies the plot, guilt over the horse and the westward voyage. The *Kalevala* supplies the image, the bard who leaves only his songs. Frodo supplies the motive, a wound that forecloses return.

Those last two are not independent witnesses. Tolkien [read the *Kalevala* as a schoolboy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Finnish_influences_on_Tolkien), called the tale of [Kullervo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kullervo) the germ of his own legendarium, and built [Quenya](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quenya) on Finnish phonology; while immersed in the poem in 1914 he wrote a mariner's voyage that scholars read as an echo of its closing scene. Both works end with a departure by ship to lands beyond the mortal world. The image and the motive belong to one family, which makes this less a motif that surfaced independently across cultures than a line that can be traced.

There is a consequence here that the film's angriest critics miss, and a limit to that consequence that its defenders miss just as often. Grief-epic is older than Homer. In [*Gilgamesh*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Epic_of_Gilgamesh), older still, the hero's quest for immortality fails, and the poem ends by pointing at the walls of [Uruk](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Uruk), because what survives a man is the work, the story. The wounded hero and the surviving song are not modern impositions on the ancient world. They predate the *Odyssey*. So the particularists are wrong to treat Nolan's ending as simply a product of 2026. But it does not follow that the ending is neutral, or that its ancestry cancels its present meaning. Nolan has [said](https://x.com/nolananalyst/status/2077838339638448407) that the film is about the idea that a society can function only through respect for the stranger, and that this truth has not changed in three thousand years. The second half of that claim, the universality, is precisely what the particularists reject, and they have a point worth stating. The specific constellation Nolan assembles, the Trojan Horse as a civilisational original sin that poisons the whole Greek world, plus a hero who disqualifies himself from his own throne, plus the moral priority of the wronged stranger, reads in 2026 as continuous with a live contemporary sensibility: inherited guilt, the toxicity of a civilisation's founding acts, the ethical centrality of the other. That last note, the toxicity of a founding victory, is one a mass audience already knows from [Villeneuve's *Dune*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dune:_Part_Two), where [Paul Atreides](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paul_Atreides)'s triumph ignites a holy war that will kill billions and traps the victor inside his own victory, though the exile that completes the pattern belongs to [Herbert's *Dune Messiah*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dune_Messiah) rather than to any film yet released, and [Villeneuve's third instalment](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dune:_Part_Three) is due in December. An old image can carry a current argument, and this one does. Both things are true at once. The materials are canonical and the selection is contemporary, and neither observation defeats the other.

The same legibility runs through the ending's emotional shape, and here the nearest model is Nolan's own. [*The Dark Knight Rises*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Dark_Knight_Rises) closes on a hero who takes the fall for a necessary deception, stages his own death, leaves the city to a successor, and rides off with a companion, which is to say Nolan had already filmed this ending once and is mythologising it again. Behind it stands an older popular form, the Western gunslinger who cleans up the town and then rides on because the violence that saved the community has stained him out of any place in it. [*Shane*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shane_%28film%29) is the pure case, and [*Logan*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Logan_%28film%29) is its elegiac late descendant, the broken protector who dies guarding the child who carries the future and who quotes *Shane* over the grave to say there is no going back. That is the guilt-disqualification beat, Frodo's slot again, in the vocabulary of the multiplex. Its familiarity is part of why Nolan's ending feels earned to viewers who have never opened Dante or the *Kalevala*, and it is a reminder that the switch does popular work as much as literary work: the turn toward Virgil and Dante arrives wearing a costume the audience already trusts.

## Why "fidelity" is the wrong charge

This is where the *Kalevala* does a second kind of work, and where the purist case starts to fray, though not in the way a triumphant universalist, who takes the poem to be common property that any age may remake, would like. It is the complete answer to [Dvorstone](https://x.com/dvorstone)'s [argument](https://x.com/dvorstone/status/2078339767071105355) that a sacred original must not be altered, because the *Kalevala* is itself an alteration. [Elias Lönnrot](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Elias_Lönnrot) did not transcribe one poem. He assembled and edited a national epic out of the variant songs of many singers, shaping the arc and composing the bridges. The epic that defines Finnish identity is a nineteenth-century editorial construction laid over an oral tradition. That is exactly what [the classicists say about Homer](https://variety.com/2026/film/news/christopher-nolan-the-odyssey-adaptation-scholars-react-1236815190/): [Gregory Nagy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gregory_Nagy) notes there is no single original *Odyssey* in the modern sense, because the poem came out of oral performance, and [Richard Martin](https://profiles.stanford.edu/55908) goes further, saying Homer's *Odyssey* was already historical fiction when it was composed, reimagining an ancient past of its own. There was never a fixed doctrine to betray. A careful purist can object that he never demanded fidelity to a lost original, only to the text we have, which has been stable since [the Alexandrian editors](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aristarchus_of_Samothrace). Fair, but the objection concedes the point that matters: what he defends is itself one edition of a plural tradition, a fixing rather than the fixed. [Arikan](https://letterboxd.com/aliarikan/) puts the consequence better: the poem "has no purists, only tellers", and "each retelling is really a confession". He continues with examples: "Virgil confessed empire's need to launder its origins; Dante his terror of knowledge without limits; [Joyce](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/James_Joyce) his faith that a decent cuckold walking home is epic enough; Tennyson his grief". The question worth asking of any version is therefore not whether this is Odysseus, but who is holding the mirror.

So Dvorstone's elegant formula, that universalising the poem is a reverse alchemy turning gold into lead, proves too much if it is taken as an absolute prohibition. [Joyce](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/James_Joyce) universalised and domesticated the *Odyssey* about as radically as anyone could, and [*Ulysses*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ulysses_%28novel%29) is a summit of the canon. The *Kalevala* itself universalised scattered charms into a nation's myth. The fact of remaking is not the crime. But conceding this does not turn the particularist worry into a simple error, and it would be too convenient to let it. Dvorstone is not really objecting to remaking as such. He is objecting to a particular direction of remaking, one that files down the strangeness that gives the poem its power, and that objection survives the point about oral tradition intact. What the *Kalevala* refutes is the claim that any alteration is desecration. It leaves standing the sharper claim, that this alteration trades away something the older text was uniquely good at. Admitting the first does not require dismissing the second, and the philology of Panagiotou and Kyrios is precise, which is its strength. The right move is to put the charge on the correct axis, not to wave it away.

## The real loss: polyphony

What is actually lost, once the false charges are cleared away, is neither fidelity nor some imagined purity. It is polyphony. Homer's genius is his refusal of a single verdict. Kyrios reads this beautifully himself. The [Cyclops](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polyphemus) episode poses a question, what does hospitality owe a host who has already broken it, and declines to answer, and the sin there is not the blinding but the boast afterward, the reach for [kleos](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kleos) past a limit, which is what earns [Poseidon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Poseidon)'s wrath. [The poem ends open](https://www.poetryintranslation.com/PITBR/Greek/Odyssey24.php), with more avengers on their way before [Athena](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Athena) intervenes. Restoration in Homer is bloody, partial, unfinished. Nolan collapses that irreducible ambiguity into one clean thesis in the shape of guilt: one transgression, a civilisation poisoned, a man sentenced by himself. The scene I began with is the purest case. Homer's Penelope listens to a beautiful lie and may or may not see through it; the poem never says. Nolan's Penelope receives a confession. Between those two scenes lies everything this post is trying to describe. [Wilson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Emily_Wilson_%28classicist%29) told the *Sunday Times* that the film [did not land emotionally for her at all](https://www.theguardian.com/film/2026/jul/27/odyssey-translator-emily-wilson-attacks-nolan-blockbuster), and that it gave her no reason to care whether the man got home. I was moved and she was not, and the likeliest reason is that she was measuring the scene against what the poem does with it. It is, as Kyrios notes, precisely his method elsewhere, a simple moral in baroque wrapping, the same move that reduces [*Interstellar*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Interstellar_%28film%29), [*Inception*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inception), and [*Oppenheimer*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oppenheimer_%28film%29) to a single legible message.

The choice of stream is not neutral, and this is where the particularists' deeper anxiety earns its hearing. Homer's openness and the restorative *nostos* tradition are not simply one option among interchangeable equals. In the present climate they are the harder option, the one that keeps the alien value-world at a distance where it can still unsettle a modern viewer. Choosing the guilt stream instead does something specific: it domesticates. It resolves the poem's tensions in the direction of a sensibility the audience already holds, and it quietly retires the confrontation with a world that measured worth by cunning, honour, and vengeance rather than by guilt and empathy. That is exactly the loss Dvorstone gestures at, and it is a real one, not a phantom conjured by nostalgia.

Underneath the moral exchange lies an aesthetic one. The Homeric world holds that the quasi-divine beauty and excellence of a [Helen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Helen_of_Troy) or an [Achilles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Achilles) can dignify the carnage they set in motion, so that the blood spilled on the plain at Troy reads as the founding sacrifice of a civilisation rather than as a crime. [Nietzsche](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Friedrich_Nietzsche) made that premise explicit in [*The Birth of Tragedy*](https://www.gutenberg.org/files/51356/51356-h/51356-h.htm), whose most repeated formula is that the world stands justified only as an aesthetic phenomenon, and whose tragic spirit affirms suffering rather than seeking redemption from it. He was candid about the opponent, treating Christianity throughout with a pointed silence. The Christian premise refuses the trade, since suffering is redeemed by innocence and atonement and never by beauty. Nolan does not simply reverse Homer's verdict on his hero. He declines the older claim that excellence can justify what it costs, and the film's tone follows it down, brutal where the poem is noble.

Here the three factual errors Panagiotou catalogues stop looking like errors and start looking like tools. [Zeus does not hate Odysseus](https://www.poetryintranslation.com/PITBR/Greek/Odyssey1.php); Athena defends him in the poem's opening, and it is Poseidon who is wrathful, over the blinding of [Polyphemus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polyphemus). Odysseus never ate the lotus; his men did, on a different island. Most tellingly, [Calypso did not want him gone](https://www.poetryintranslation.com/PITBR/Greek/Odyssey5.php): she offered to make him immortal and ageless if he would stay, and he chose a mortal home anyway, a choice that is one of the poem's philosophical pillars and one the film simply deletes. Each of these serves the same thesis. That is the signature of a thesis, not a slip. The unglamorous explanation deserves its hearing first. Three hours, a studio, many hands on a script: long static scenes are exactly what gets trimmed, and any one of these changes could be an accident of compression. What resists that reading is their direction. Accidents scatter; these all point the same way, and replacing Anticleia and Achilles in the underworld with an invented Virgilian is a trade, not a trim.

The same logic reaches down into the machinery of the plot. In Homer the disguise works only because [Athena transforms him](https://www.poetryintranslation.com/PITBR/Greek/Odyssey13.php), withering his skin and dimming his eyes so that not even his household knows him. Nolan will not allow the miracle, and as [Ali Arikan](https://letterboxd.com/aliarikan/) [notes](https://letterboxd.com/aliarikan/film/the-odyssey-2026/), he blinds [Eumaeus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eumaeus) instead, re-rigging the mechanism so the story can run without gods. It is the whole argument in one plot fix. Where the poem needs a goddess, the film needs only an infirmity, and the divine order that Homer's world runs on becomes scenery that the story no longer requires.

It also forces a correction I have to make to my own instinct. It is flattering to call the film a conversation with Homer conducted in Homer's own material. It is not, quite. The accuracy in certain places (xenia, the recognition, the singers) does not make the whole a sophisticated reinterpretation so much as a more effective vehicle for Nolan's recurring obsession. Many of the changes are not delicate commentary. They are dramaturgical simplifications pulling in one direction. The betrayal, if there is one, is not of Homer's values but of his method, many voices exchanged for one readable message. This is also, quietly, why even sympathetic classicists find the film narrow. [Joel Christensen](https://www.brandeis.edu/about/administration/christensen.html) [calls it, of all things, conservative](https://variety.com/2026/film/news/christopher-nolan-the-odyssey-adaptation-scholars-react-1236815190/), and notes how constrained the women's roles are. That verdict is worth pausing on, because it arrives from more than one direction. [Bret Devereaux](https://foreignpolicy.com/author/bret-devereaux/), a historian of antiquity who takes the film as a pointed intervention in the arguments of the present, still [ends up describing](https://foreignpolicy.com/2026/07/23/odyssey-civilization-collapse-history/) its moral architecture as almost [Burkean](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Edmund_Burke): catastrophe follows not from a society's structural injustices but from the violation of inherited norms. Two readers coming at the film for quite different reasons reach the same structural description, and when that happens the description is probably about the film rather than about either reader.

The last word on narrowness belongs to the film's own source. [Emily Wilson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Emily_Wilson_%28classicist%29), whose translation reportedly informed the screenplay and whose celebrated first line renders *polytropos*, the man of many turns, as "complicated man," [finds the result neither complicated nor about a complicated man](https://www.derekthompson.org/p/emily-wilsons-complicated-feelings). What [Damon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Matt_Damon) plays, in her description, is "an action hero who feels bad about being an action hero." Two qualifications about my chief witness. Her verdict has escalated from the measured tone of that interview to a full polemic that includes the line "[I would be ashamed to have written any part of this script](https://www.lrb.co.uk/the-paper/v48/n14/emily-wilson/an-uncomplicated-man)", and it is written by someone whose translation was used in the publicity and whose reading of the poem she considers betrayed. That bears on the weight of her verdicts, though not on her observations, which are checkable against the film. She also calls the film's narrative structure gimmicky, where I have called its storytelling frame near-perfect. On that we simply disagree. The scheming has been stripped out along with the comedy: no Cyclops name-trick, no delight in disguise, not even a scene of the Horse being built. The film's tech hero agonises at length over what his invention did and is never once shown inventing. The man the poem names for his turns has had the turns removed. She has since made the case at length, in a review for the *London Review of Books* titled "[An Uncomplicated Man](https://www.lrb.co.uk/the-paper/v48/n14/emily-wilson/an-uncomplicated-man)", which states the whole argument in three words. Her central charge there is that the film has nothing convincing to say about time, memory, history or war, nor about the relation between one warrior's glorious homecoming and the lives of his family, his comrades, his neighbours and his adversaries. That last complaint is the polyphony argument in her own terms: a hero's return means something only when the poem keeps counting what it cost everyone else.

She catches a paradox, too, that cuts deeper than any philological complaint. A film organised around guilt toward the victims never lets the victims be people. Wilson describes its Trojans as fighting in masks, faceless as machinery. The word is loose: they wear helmets on the [Corinthian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Corinthian_helmet) pattern, which cover much of the face but leave the mouth and chin in view, and the film [opens the design further than the historical original](https://talesoftimesforgotten.com/2026/07/23/an-ancient-historians-breakdown-of-christopher-nolans-the-odyssey/) so that its actors can be seen. The effect she names is real all the same, and it is not confined to the Trojans, since Devereaux calls Agamemnon "an almost entirely faceless, frequently voiceless force of destruction" and Agamemnon leads the Greeks. What the film withholds is not the enemy's humanity in particular but almost anyone's: nearly everyone who is not Odysseus is reduced to a function. The poem the film corrects is the more humane document. Homer gives [the Cyclops a favourite ram](https://www.poetryintranslation.com/PITBR/Greek/Odyssey9.php), spoken to with tenderness by the blinded monster, and the *Iliad* does not end on Greek triumph at all but on [Hector](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hector)'s funeral, the enemy mourned at full length. The epic that supposedly glorifies the victors keeps insisting that the defeated are human. The film that indicts the victors forgets it. And if the poem can both dignify the carnage and weep for it, both Nietzsche's Homer and Wilson's, that is no inconsistency here; it is the polyphony this essay is about.

## What Nolan gets right

Fairness requires the other column, or the essay tips into the one-sidedness it is diagnosing. The *Odyssey* is among the most self-conscious works in literature about storytelling itself. It is full of singers, and its hero is a professional narrator of his own life. Nolan puts exactly this at the centre. The film [opens with a man on a table in a banquet hall, telling a story](https://www.rogerebert.com/reviews/the-odyssey-christopher-nolan-matt-damon-film-review-2026), a near-perfect frame for a film about how stories deceive and free us. Much of the picture is a tale Odysseus is telling about himself, and at one point the suitors are sung the tale of Troy, as if the *Iliad* had broken out inside the *Odyssey*. The frame also closes as it opened. [The film ends](https://foreignpolicy.com/2026/07/23/odyssey-civilization-collapse-history/) with Odysseus telling Penelope that later generations will need bards, because they will no longer be able to write, and the history behind the line is real: Greek writing was lost in the collapse and returned four centuries later on an alphabet borrowed from [Phoenician](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greek_alphabet). Nolan's last note therefore depicts the birth of the condition that produced Homer. The oral tradition whose openness no single author ever had to resolve begins where the film leaves off, which is, by accident or design, its shrewdest gesture. Here [Travis Scott](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Travis_Scott)'s bard is not desecration but insight: the oral epic singer and the improvising rapper are both keepers of memory and reputation, and the analogy is defensible even where it stings. Whatever else it does, the film sends people back to the poem, and Christensen expects fuller Greek classes. That is not nothing.

Notice, too, where the real disagreement lies. Herod Priovolos, in the most humane of the posts, reads the monsters as Nolan does, the Cyclops as power without justice, the [Sirens](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Siren_%28mythology%29) as the temptation to abandon one's purpose, [Circe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Circe) as the loss of one's humanity, [Calypso](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Calypso_%28mythology%29) as immortality turned prison, and insists that [Ithaca](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Homer%27s_Ithaca) is not an island but the place that remembers you when the world no longer knows your face. He and Nolan share the allegorical method. What Herod demands is only that the arc end in [recognition and return, Penelope and the olive-tree bed](https://www.poetryintranslation.com/PITBR/Greek/Odyssey23.php), rather than in renunciation. The quarrel is not about method. It is about the verdict that homecoming is finally allowed to reach.

## Could it have been otherwise?

The strongest case for the switch is not that Homer sanctions it but that a film cannot do what the poem does. The *Odyssey*'s openness is partly a property of its making. A work with [no single author](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Homeric_Question), assembled across generations of performance, can hold contradictory verdicts because nobody ever had to choose between them. A single feature with one runtime and one dramatic arc has to choose, and the choosing may be a cost of the form rather than evidence of a flattened sensibility. On that reading, what I have called domestication is simply what adaptation into drama looks like, and the fair comparison is not Homer but other films. There is a Homeric case for the anti-war reading too. The poem does count what Troy cost, in the dead companions, the twenty lost years, and [the ghosts of Book 11](https://www.poetryintranslation.com/PITBR/Greek/Odyssey11.php), and a film that makes that reckoning its spine is developing something the poem already contains rather than importing it wholesale.

The poem even contains, in miniature, the film's whole point of view. [In Book 8, at the Phaeacian court](https://www.poetryintranslation.com/PITBR/Greek/Odyssey8.php), Odysseus asks the bard to sing of the wooden horse, his own greatest triumph, and then weeps at it, and Homer reaches for an extraordinary simile: he cries like a woman flinging herself on her husband's body as her city falls, feeling the spears at her back as she is led into slavery. For the length of one comparison, Homer gives his victor the eyes of his victims. A defender of the film can fairly say that Nolan has not imported Virgil at all; he has taken a famous and devastating simile of Homer's and made a whole film inside it. The answer is the essay's whole argument in one sentence. Homer gives the victim's eyes for the length of a simile, inside a poem that still gives Odysseus his hall; Nolan makes the simile the verdict of the entire film. A voice became the thesis.

The claim that the form forced the choice has an answer, though, and Wilson supplies it. It is not kind. Asked directly whether Homer's Odysseus is simply unfilmable, given a blockbuster audience's low tolerance for ambiguity, she refused the premise. The past quarter century of screen culture has run on ambiguous men, con artists and gangster patriarchs whom audiences follow gladly, and the proof sits inside this very cast: Damon himself played [the talented Mr. Ripley](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Talented_Mr._Ripley_%28film%29) back in 1999, lying his way across the sea, which is to say the production demonstrably had an actor who can do cunning, hired him, and declined the performance. In Wilson's judgment Nolan reached for an easy idealisation when a notch more complexity was available and the viewer could have been trusted with it. If she is right, the flattening was not the price of adaptation. It was the purchase. Her examples cut with less force than she implies, since sixty hours of television can afford an ambiguity that a three-hour epic compressing twenty-four books may not. But Ripley survives the objection: two hours, one film, the same actor.

Why Nolan wanted this particular purchase has a simple answer, and it has nothing to do with the politics of 2026. He had made the film already. Arikan reads *The Odyssey* as the second panel of a diptych with *Oppenheimer*: the Horse treated exactly as the bomb was, a device that succeeded past its inventor's intention, leaving him to live inside what he built and watch his ingenuity become everybody's weapon. The film closes the loop by staging the killing of the suitors as a sack repeated indoors, the same deceit and the same slaughter, so that the method's author meets it again under his own roof. On that account the switch is not an imported ideology but an auteur's subject, the same story told twice, about "the man who wins the war and loses the account of himself". This is the same habit the polyphony section counted against him, one legible message inside elaborate machinery, seen now from the other side, where it looks like fidelity to a lifelong subject.

The strongest objection to the film is likewise formal rather than political, and Arikan makes it. The bomb was a genuine discontinuity, with arithmetic and body counts behind it, so, as he puts it, "the guilt was in the archive before it was in the screenplay". The Horse has no such standing and must be raised to it, a stratagem reclassified as a sin by directorial fiat, and that reclassification has Roman precedent, since Virgil made it first, on behalf of Troy's heirs rather than its sackers. A film working in symbols could have borne the reclassification, since a symbol will carry any weight placed on it. But Nolan's *Odyssey* is the most physical ever filmed, real water and real rivets, and his Poseidon has dwindled into a word men reach for when the sea turns against them. Literalism, in Arikan's phrase, is a contract: it buys immediacy and it costs allegory. Three hours of sweat and timber cannot then ask their centrepiece to be a metaphysical event. There is a historical objection too, and it is harder to answer than the philological ones. Cities burned and altars were fouled for centuries before the first soldier was hidden inside a wooden animal, and Arikan runs the litany of every age holding the same funeral for valour: the Church over the crossbow, the story told of the samurai and the musket, an Anatolian folk lament that valour left the world when the rifle entered it. A fall that recurs at every upgrade in weaponry is a strange sort of fall. The contract breaks a second time at the level of history. Nolan grounds his collapse in the real Late Bronze Age, the Sea Peoples named and the ruin dated, but [historians of that collapse](https://foreignpolicy.com/2026/07/23/odyssey-civilization-collapse-history/) describe a system failing on many fronts at once: climatic shifts destabilising centralised palace economies, the weakest states on the periphery going first, trade flows breaking, and the resulting waves of refugees and raiders bringing down the rest in a domino. Nolan's cause is single, and it is moral. The reduction traced here in the poem operates on the history too.

## Casting, heritage, and a category error

The casting fight should be kept apart from the thematic one, because fusing them corrupts both. [Claire Lehmann](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Claire_Lehmann) [is right](https://x.com/clairlemon/status/2078258509058371709) that demanding realistic casting for a film with a one-eyed monster who eats men like sweets and a witch who turns men into pigs is a category error, and realism was never the point. The film's departures from Greek descent were not confined to its most-discussed roles. Odysseus himself, the Greek of Greeks, is played by [Matt Damon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Matt_Damon), a Boston actor of Finnish descent whose maternal family name was Pajari before it was anglicised to Paige, no more Greek by blood than the Black Helen, and almost no one else in the cast is Greek either. Descent, in other words, was never the standard the film applied to anyone. Dvorstone is right that the deeper objection is not realism but something else, though Joyce and the *Kalevala* already answer the strong form of it. And there is a legitimate core to the Greek frustration that neither dismissal touches. A living culture is watching a [$250-million production shoot at its heritage sites and take public money](https://djhdcj.substack.com/p/nolans-odyssey) while its own claim on the material is treated as sentimental. That is not the same as the demand that only Greeks may play Greeks, and it should not be collapsed into it. [The open letters made the reasonable version of the case](https://www.comicbasics.com/did-nolans-the-odyssey-get-ancient-greeks-right-the-casting-debate-explained/): Greek and Greek Cypriot writers pointing out that Greeks have not vanished, that they remain a living people with a continuous if transformed relationship to these stories, and that such a people has a real interest when a foreign industry turns its inheritance into a global product on its soil and with its subsidies. Panagiotou's sharpest moment dramatises the sting, a scene in which a man asks raiders in Greek who they are and [a British actor of Indian descent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Himesh_Patel) answers, in English, that they are Greeks. The fair reply is [Dimitris Plantzos](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dimitris_Plantzos)'s, [that no one owns antiquity](https://balkaninsight.com/2026/07/08/nolans-black-helen-of-troy-shifts-us-culture-wars-to-ancient-greece/bi/) and that Helen was always a poetic construction every age remakes. Both of these are true, and the honest position holds them together rather than using one to silence the other. What matters for the film's meaning is that this is a question about ownership and standing, not about fidelity, and it deserves to be argued on its own terms.

There is, however, one respect in which casting and theme legitimately touch, and [Johnathan Bi](https://x.com/johnathanbi) [has identified it](https://x.com/johnathanbi/status/2079613102975279459). Xenia is a boundary institution. It presupposes a sharp line between the household and the stranger, and what it asks is not modern moral symmetry but something asymmetrical, owed to the guest because he stands under Zeus's protection and might be a god. A world in which that line has already dissolved gives the institution no work to do. Bi's objection is therefore not to who plays whom but to what the crew implies about the world: if men from across the known earth sit unremarkably among Odysseus's Ithacans, then the map is already drawn, the contact has already happened, and the sea has stopped being strange. What is lost is mystery, the sense that any figure on any shore might turn out to be a host, a monster, or a god. The tell that the argument is structural rather than racial is what Bi says he would have preferred: an all Black, Indian or Chinese cast rather than the mixed one the film gave him. The three are interchangeable in his argument. Uniformity is not, because only an unmixed crew leaves the boundary in place.

The argument has a soft spot worth naming. Homer's boundaries are not racial ones. They run between household and outsider, Greek speaker and [barbarian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Barbarian), mortal and god, and his Mediterranean is already busy with Egyptians and [Phoenicians](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Phoenicia), as [Odysseus's own invented Cretan tales](https://www.poetryintranslation.com/PITBR/Greek/Odyssey14.php) attest. Treating visible difference as the marker of Homeric strangeness is itself a modern substitution. What survives the correction is the structural claim, and it is a real one. A world feels large when its outsiders are legible as outsiders, and this is the same unhooking traced earlier, now visible in the casting rather than in the script: an obligation that once ran across a boundary becomes one that presumes no boundary at all.

## The dividing line

Here is where it settles. The film does not honour the *Odyssey*'s themes and it does not miss their point. It switches them, Homer's tributary for Virgil's and the ending for Dante's, so that the cunning, restored king becomes a guilty founder who sails west. That current runs deep enough to surface in the Grey Havens and in Väinämöinen's copper boat. Its true cost is not fidelity but polyphony, Homer's refusal of a verdict exchanged for a single legible sentence of inherited guilt.

Whether that is a profound re-contesting or a borrowing of authority to say something close to the poem's opposite depends on a prior question, and the question is a real one on which reasonable people divide. Are the founding stories of the West a patrimony, to be handed on as intact as possible so that their strangeness keeps its power to challenge us? Or are they common property, which each generation is entitled to fight over and remake? Both answers have a serious tradition behind them, and each carries a cost. The patrimony instinct risks the museum, a reverence that goes lifeless and reduces a living poem to an object under glass. The remaking instinct risks the opposite, taming the foreign so efficiently that it stops challenging anyone, which is what Dvorstone means by gold turned to lead. Neither cost is imaginary, and neither side has a monopoly on the poem.

One thing should be said plainly, because it is usually left out. In 2026 the remaking side is not the underdog. It commands the studios, most of the prestige press, and a large part of the academy. When a $250-million film backed by that apparatus remakes a foundational text in the image of the present, it is the powerful rewriting the inherited, not the insurgent freeing it. The asymmetry runs one way in institutions, though, not in reach: the reaction commanded platforms of its own, enough to make the film a public quarrel months before most people had seen it. That does not make the remake illegitimate, and it does not vindicate the reaction, some of which curdled into ugliness that deserves no defence. But it does mean the particularists are not merely nostalgists guarding a museum. They are also pointing, clumsily and sometimes badly, at a genuine asymmetry of power over who gets to decide what the old stories mean.

So the film is best understood as what it is, a powerful, coherent, characteristically Nolan work that carries Homer's name and speaks, for long stretches, in another tradition's voice, the voice of Virgil and Dante and Milton rather than Homer. That is not ignorance. It should be said that this post argues against the grain. The film is [a large commercial success](https://www.boxofficemojo.com/title/tt33764258/) and most reviewers have praised it; the dissent has come mainly from classicists. Wilson, the harshest of them, still calls the release an event to celebrate, notes that translations are selling, and says she is grateful that Nolan is getting the public reading again. It is a choice, made from a position of enormous cultural power, and its price is precisely the polyphony Homer left open. The real dispute *The Odyssey* has provoked was never the colour of [Helen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Helen_of_Troy)'s skin. It is whether an inherited story is ours to keep or ours to remake, and who the "ours" in that sentence turns out to be. On that question, and not on the casting, the film should be judged.
