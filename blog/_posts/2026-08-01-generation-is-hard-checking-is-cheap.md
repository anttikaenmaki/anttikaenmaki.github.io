Speculation about artificial intelligence and mathematics is cheap, and most of it is worth what it costs. What has changed over the past year is that the question acquired evidence: benchmark results produced under controlled conditions and refereed by human beings, first-person reports from mathematicians who have used the tools on real papers, and enough institutional response to argue with rather than merely forecast.

This is the first of five posts that try to do two things the surrounding discussion mostly does not. The first is to compare the change with the earlier mechanisations of mathematical labour, symbolic and numerical computation above all, since that is where most of the analogies in circulation go wrong, and they go wrong in both directions. The second is to supply a mechanism where the best existing accounts offer an observation.

Two boundaries, stated at the outset rather than discovered later. This is about AI changing how mathematics is done. It is not about the quite different argument, made by national academies and increasingly by departments defending their budgets, that mathematics is the substrate on which AI is built and should be funded accordingly; that is the burden of the UK [Academy for the Mathematical Sciences](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Academy_for_the_Mathematical_Sciences)' recent [primer on the mathematics underpinning AI](https://www.acadmathsci.org.uk/maths-in-the-age-of-ai-why-the-mathematical-sciences-matter-more-than-ever/). Both arguments are sound and they point in opposite emotional directions, one saying the discipline is threatened and the other that it has never mattered more. They are about different things, and running them together produces the impression of special pleading. The second will shape budgets. The first will shape what it is like to do the job.

The other boundary is evidential. What follows rests on a small number of visible accounts: a controlled benchmark, two practitioner reports, the public reflections of a handful of unusually engaged mathematicians. There is at present no representative survey of what working mathematicians actually do with these tools, in which subfields, with what failure rates. That absence should be felt as a real limitation and not papered over. The institutional argument these posts build towards is therefore stronger than the empirical map it sits on: it predicts what incentives will do, which is a different and easier thing than describing what is currently happening across the discipline.

A caution before any of it. The mathematicians who convened the [Leiden workshop on mechanisation and mathematical research](https://www.lorentzcenter.nl/mechanization-and-mathematical-research.html) in September 2025 were struck by the sheer breadth of view among their participants: some delighted at the prospect of being freed from the tedium of proof-writing, others convinced that precisely that tedium carries something essential to the craft; some arguing for a far larger role for formal proof in research and teaching, others for the exact opposite. [Reporting the workshop in the *Notices*](https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.24914), Johan Commelin, Mateja Jamnik, Rodrigo Ochigame, Lenny Taelman and [Akshay Venkatesh](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Akshay_Venkatesh) conclude that simplistic generalisations about what mathematicians value should be treated with suspicion, and note the standing gap between professed values and actual practice. Almost everything written in this genre, including these posts, generalises about the discipline's values in order to say anything at all. Take what follows as claims about incentives and institutions, which are observable, rather than about what mathematicians care for, which is not.

Since the five posts can be read separately, here is the whole argument in outline. Two mechanisms do the work, and they are independent of one another.

The first is about tasks. Inside a formal system, checking a proof is decidable while finding one is not, so verification is cheap and generation is expensive; but that asymmetry holds only where a formal criterion exists, and in informal mathematics the checker is a person reading for a week. Where generation is cheap, machine output will therefore be absorbed without friction wherever checking is cheap, and will cause institutional trouble wherever it is not, and the line between those two runs through subfields and through the individual tasks of a single paper rather than between disciplines. That is the subject of this post.

The second is about institutions. The goals of mathematics have been correlated because they shared one scarce input, the trained attention of experts, and because they were joint products of the same activity: solving a problem yielded understanding, training and canonical form at no additional cost. Cheap generation decouples one node of that network and leaves the others where they were. The proxies by which we assess each other stop tracking what they used to track, the cheap heuristics that tell us what to read carefully stop carrying information, and the scarce factor moves to verification and digestion, which nobody is paid to do. That is the subject of the third post, [When the proxies stop measuring](/blog/when-the-proxies-stop-measuring/).

Neither mechanism entails the other. If digestion were cheap, the first would hold and the second would not. They meet where both are true, and that is where the conclusion sits: correct results accumulating faster than anyone can absorb them, in a literature with no working way of deciding what deserves attention. Between the two mechanisms sits [What mathematics has already survived](/blog/what-mathematics-has-already-survived/), which tests the comparison against the mechanisations the discipline has absorbed before; after them come [Where the argument bends](/blog/where-the-argument-bends/), which points the framework at applied mathematics and at one subfield in detail, and [What to do about proof abundance](/blog/what-to-do-about-proof-abundance/), which sets out what follows for practice, publishing, teaching and institutions.

This post establishes the framework the rest uses. It sets out what these systems have demonstrably done as of August 2026, distinguishes two properties of the task being automated, and argues that one of them predicts where the technology is absorbed without friction and where it causes institutional trouble. It also states, and then repairs, the version of that claim that is refuted by the history of proof assistants.

## Where the evidence stands

Fixing the state of play comes before speculating, because the speculation is only as good as the base rate it starts from. Every figure here is a snapshot, and the snapshot has already moved once during the writing: most of this section was drafted in July 2026, and the largest single item in it arrived on the first of August. That is worth recording rather than smoothing over, since the rate at which a post of this kind dates is itself evidence about the thing it describes.

The cleanest evidence is the [First Proof project](https://1stproof.org/). Its [second batch](https://arxiv.org/html/2606.18119v1) took ten unpublished research-level lemmas, each with a known human proof of at most eight pages, and ran four systems centrally under controlled conditions with a one-shot, twenty-four-hour budget: a commercial model together with three academic harnesses. The outputs were then refereed anonymously by [about thirty mathematicians over two days](https://current.fas.harvard.edu/stories/first-proofs-second-batch-math-problems-test-ai) at Harvard's Center of Mathematical Sciences and Applications, and the results were [presented on 10 June](https://cmsa.fas.harvard.edu/event/fp_61026/). Seven of the ten problems were solved at publication-level quality by at least one system, at compute costs ranging from roughly ten to a thousand dollars per problem. Two months earlier, in a [preliminary screen](https://1stproof.org/assets/docs/report.pdf), the same problems had been put to the plain frontier models with a thirty-minute timeout, and *none of the ten fell*. The entire delta, at present, lies between "chat with a model" and "run a research harness for a day."

Two details of the referee reports matter more than the headline. Exposition failed in a characteristic way: pedantic where nothing was at stake, thin where the real idea lived, with overviews that did not help the reader. And attribution failed systematically, with referees flagging missing citations, wrong citations, and hallucinated ones across submissions. Two things are moving here at very different speeds, and the difference matters more than either rate. The apparatus that establishes *correctness* improved discontinuously over the following eight weeks, as the August announcement below shows. The apparatus that situates a result in a literature, which is to say knowing what is genuinely new, whose theorem is being used and which other problems the result bears on, did not move at all.

The earlier, informal [first batch in February](https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.05192) gave a messier picture, and the disagreement about it is instructive. [Scientific American reported the results as decidedly mixed](https://www.scientificamerican.com/article/first-proof-is-ais-toughest-math-test-yet-the-results-are-mixed/), with experts poking holes in claimed solutions almost as soon as they appeared, while [OpenAI](https://openai.com/index/first-proof-submissions/) and a [Google DeepMind team reported six autonomous solutions out of ten](https://math.berkeley.edu/~fengt/FirstProof.pdf) under their own reading of the rules. The gap between those accounts is not mainly about mathematics; it is about what counts as autonomy and what counts as a solution.

Then, on 1 August 2026, that became the second-cleanest evidence. OpenAI [published a report](https://openai.com/index/ten-advances-in-mathematics/) claiming that an internal version of its next model, named Astra, had produced new results on ten problems in mathematics and theoretical computer science, each of which had seen no progress on its central question for at least a decade and in most cases far longer. The claimed results run across group theory, high-dimensional geometry, coding theory, arithmetic circuit complexity, quantum complexity, lattice cryptography and extremal combinatorics. The headline item is [an explicit construction of a non-sofic group](https://thenextweb.com/news/openai-astra-model-ten-math-proofs-non-sofic-groups), settling a question open since Gromov introduced soficity in 1999, alongside a disproof of Connes's rigidity conjecture, a proof of Ehrhart's volume conjecture, new bounds for high-dimensional sphere packing and the resolution of several problems posed by Erdős. The work came as a 249-page manuscript with machine-checkable Lean 4 certificates for every result published on GitHub, and OpenAI reports the [total compute cost for all ten at roughly two thousand dollars](https://www.forbes.com/sites/jonmarkman/2026/08/03/openais-astra-solved-10-decades-old-math-problems-for-just-2000/). Thomas Bloom, who runs the Erdős problems database, called it big news and judged it more significant than the unit-distance disproof of May.

The caveats are real and should be stated in the same breath. The model is not publicly available, so nothing about the process can be independently reproduced. The report is authored by the company whose product it evaluates, and lists that company rather than any person as its author. Critics have raised the obvious question about problem selection, since ten results chosen for announcement out of an unknown number attempted is a filtered sample by construction. And a company announcement is precisely the mode of publication [the Leiden Declaration objected to](https://leidendeclaration.ai/).

But one thing distinguishes this from every previous announcement of the kind, and it is the thing this series is about. The results ship with Lean certificates. Whatever one thinks of the framing, the mathematics can be checked by anyone who cares to, without trusting OpenAI, without waiting for a referee, and without understanding how any of it was found. That is a different epistemic object from a press release, and it is the difference the first of this series' two mechanisms turns on. The second is untouched by it and would bite even if every machine-generated proof arrived certified. If certificates were to become universal, the argument here would begin to dissolve rather than be confirmed, which is the scenario set out at the end of [the last post](/blog/what-to-do-about-proof-abundance/).

The most useful commentary came from a mathematician who had been running the same experiment independently. In [a post published the following day](https://tjoresearchnotes.wordpress.com/2026/08/02/reaping-without-sowing/), Tobias Osborne describes a pipeline he had built to swarm the literature with agents, mine explicitly stated open problems from the TeX source of published papers, rank them by measured community attention, and set attacker agents on the most promising. His figures are worth having: a statistical profile of 783 accepted talks at a quantum information conference over five years, a hindcast showing that forty per cent of the most-discussed preprints became conference talks against a base rate four times lower, sixty-two open problems mined from around a hundred papers, and eight agents assigned to the eight best candidates. Five of the eight problems were eliminated, one of them because it had already been answered twenty-five lines below where it was posed, and one because the agent attacking it constructed a rigorous counterexample to its own hypothesis. He reports having beaten one of the larger efforts to a result by a day, at five orders of magnitude less compute.

Two conclusions from that experiment matter more than the result. The first is his characterisation of what has actually fallen, which is sharper than anything in this series: the ten advances are the *harvestable* results, the conjectures that fit inside a context window and are provable in the language mathematics already has. Hence his title, reaping without sowing. The second is that measured community attention proved not merely observable but *targetable*, which is the Goodhart problem in its most concrete form yet. It is now possible to optimise directly for which open problems the profession would be most impressed to see closed.

Alongside this, one named sceptic changed his mind in public, which is the sort of event worth recording because it happens so rarely. Daniel Litt, who had argued that [AI mathematical capability was likely to remain jagged](https://epochai.substack.com/p/ai-math-capabilities-could-be-jagged), [conceded a bet](https://x.com/littmath/status/2083733224027500584) that AI would produce journal-quality number theory within five years at modest cost, noting that strictly speaking the bet had not resolved, since no such paper had yet appeared, but that he had clearly been wrong about which capabilities would be needed to produce one. He [added](https://x.com/littmath/status/2083733230168002676) that the models still seem unable to do certain kinds of high-quality research, and that he expects this too is a matter of not very much time.

One further result belongs here with a disclosure attached. Around the same period, Anthropic's Fable model was reported to have [disproved the Jacobian conjecture](https://mashable.com/tech/anthropic-fable-5-disproves-jacobian-conjecture), a claim Osborne also [notes in passing](https://tjoresearchnotes.wordpress.com/) with the qualification that the disproof was apparent rather than confirmed. I am in no position to assess it: these posts were drafted with the assistance of a model made by that company, which is exactly the kind of entanglement the disclosure norms recommended in [the last post of this series](/blog/what-to-do-about-proof-abundance/) are meant to surface. The claim should be treated with the same caution as any other announcement by a party with an interest in the answer, and it has not been used as evidence for anything argued here.

A second and much steeper evidence stream runs alongside it. [Alex Kontorovich](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alex_Kontorovich), writing [for the Congress's proceedings](https://arxiv.org/abs/2510.15924) from inside the formalisation community, sets out the competition trajectory. In 2023 no system could earn a single point at the International Mathematical Olympiad. In 2024 DeepMind's AlphaProof and AlphaGeometry [reached silver, one point short of gold](https://deepmind.google/discover/blog/ai-solves-imo-problems-at-silver-medal-level/). In 2025 several systems reached gold, roughly half of them via formal verification and half working purely in natural language, with [Gemini Deep Think solving five of six problems for thirty-five points under official grading](https://deepmind.google/blog/advanced-version-of-gemini-with-deep-think-officially-achieves-gold-medal-standard-at-the-international-mathematical-olympiad/). Writing in September 2025, he predicted that by the 2026 Olympiad the leading systems would stop competing, the contest having become trivial for them. At [this month's Olympiad in Shanghai](https://www.taipeitimes.com/News/world/archives/2026/07/24/2003861308), Huawei and Xiaohongshu each announced a perfect score under the official judging process, the first time any model has achieved full marks, against seven of the 666 human contestants who did the same. Each of these figures, though, is an announcement by the developer of the system concerned, and the aggregator listings that carry them trace back to vendor documentation rather than to independent grading. The 2025 result was certified by IMO coordinators under the criteria applied to students, and that is the standard the 2026 claims should meet before anyone treats them as settled.

The two streams were worth holding apart until quite recently, and the reason for saying so has now partly dissolved. Competition mathematics is saturated. Research mathematics at the eight-page-lemma level stood at seven in ten under a day of compute in June, with attribution and exposition failing throughout, and the inference from Olympiad gold to research capability by scaling was then an argument rather than an observation. The Astra results do not settle that argument, because a filtered sample of ten announced successes establishes a lower bound on capability and nothing at all about the rate. But they do remove the comfortable position, available as late as June, that nothing above lemma scale had fallen. Decades-old named conjectures have now fallen, with certificates. What remains genuinely unknown is the denominator.

A third stream, and the one closest to ordinary working conditions, is the practitioner report. [Michael Kearns](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Michael_Kearns_(computer_scientist)) and [Aaron Roth](https://www.amazon.science/author/aaron-roth) [describe using agentic tools](https://www.amazon.science/blog/how-ai-is-changing-the-nature-of-mathematical-research) over three weeks to produce a [fifty-page paper](https://arxiv.org/abs/2507.09683) that would ordinarily have taken months, prompting at the level of a proof sketch and letting the system supply the formal detail; along the way it proved a small lemma they had not known about, which simplified the argument they had in mind. They also give the number that matters most: the proofs come out correct perhaps three-quarters of the time. That rate is high enough to be extremely useful to an expert who can spot the failures, and low enough, in their phrase, to produce research slop when used without discernment. The models, they note, still do not know what is interesting.

Two further reports fill in that picture, and both are unusually honest about variance. DeepMind's own [account of giving working mathematicians access to an agentic system](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2605.06651) records a range of feedback and satisfaction, with some users finding the tool considerably less useful for their work than others, which is the sort of thing developer write-ups generally omit. And a [first-person case study in mathematical statistics](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2511.18828) reaches a conclusion worth taking seriously: its author judges that without many years of accumulated expertise in reading and writing proofs, in literature search and in the relevant domain, it would have been effectively impossible to use the tools properly at all, and that they therefore do not diminish the importance of education in the slightest. Both sit alongside the benchmark numbers rather than replacing them, and they point the same way. The present value of these systems is strongly conditional on the expertise of whoever is holding them.

On the other side of the ledger, [Terence Tao](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Terence_Tao)'s own reading of the [Erdős problem corpus](https://www.erdosproblems.com) is that the true success rate is a percent or two, concentrated at the easy end, and that what is being cleared is the [attention-starved long tail](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=zJvuaRVc8Bg), problems posed once and never followed up, rather than anything the community was waiting for. There is now direct evidence for that reading from the other direction. DeepMind's own [case study on the Erdős database](https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.22401) describes its work as *semi-autonomous*, emphasises literature search and human checking, and records that several problems listed as open were open only in the sense of being hard to find: a discoverability failure rather than a frontier. The [companion paper](https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.10177) makes the general point that research requires judgement about novelty, relevance and framing, not merely the production of an argument. Tao's warning about [reporting bias against negative results](https://mathstodon.xyz/@tao/115911902186528812) is the right prior: we see the wins.

His [public lecture at the International Congress of Mathematicians in Philadelphia](https://www.mathunion.org/icm/icm-2026) sidesteps the capability question altogether, assuming a strong form of it and asking what follows *conditionally*. That is the right methodological move, and [the third post of this series](/blog/when-the-proxies-stop-measuring/) takes it up at length; nothing in the framework below depends on how good these systems turn out to be.

## Specification, and the cost of checking

Nearly every difference between this wave and its predecessors falls out of two properties of the task being automated.

The first is *specification*. Every earlier automation of mathematical labour attacked a task with a complete formal specification. Compute a sine to ten digits. Factor a polynomial. Solve a linear system. Sum a hypergeometric series in closed form. Such a tool can be wrong, but *wrong* is defined, and, importantly, the failure modes are systematic: round-off, catastrophic cancellation, ill-conditioning, branch-cut conventions, unstated genericity assumptions inside a simplification. Systematic failure modes can be taught. A generation of numerical analysts learned backward error analysis; a generation of [Mathematica](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wolfram_Mathematica) users learned not to trust an automatic simplification of an expression with radicals. The profession absorbed each tool by absorbing its error structure.

Language models are the first mathematical instrument pointed at tasks with *no* specification at all: find a proof, find the right lemma, find the right level of generality, judge whether this is interesting. The price of dropping the specification is that the failure mode stops being systematic and becomes *plausible*. An incorrect step from a computer algebra system looks like an artefact of the system; an incorrect step from a model looks like a correct step. There is no error theory to teach, and the errors are in effect optimised against precisely the surface features a human reviewer uses to decide where to spend attention.

Stated that way the axis sounds like a property of the instrument, and it is not. A model searching for a proof of a stated goal in Lean is performing a fully specified task, and it is the same technology. What is unspecified is not the machine's work but the decisions around it: which problem to attack, what level of generality is right, whether the result is worth having.

> Specification is not a fact about the tool but a setting the user chooses. The axis measures how much unspecified judgment is delegated, which makes it a dial rather than a property.

That converts the practical question from what these systems are like into where one sets the dial, and every piece of practical advice in [the last post of this series](/blog/what-to-do-about-proof-abundance/) is a rule about that setting.

Tao's [image of the tool as a probability kernel rather than a function](https://mathstodon.xyz/@tao/109971907648866712), an output distribution concentrated near the right answer but carrying subtle and plausible error, is the same observation, and it is what licenses all of his practical advice: [prefer red-teaming to blue-teaming](https://mathstodon.xyz/@tao/114915604830689046), and [rely on the output only where you could independently red-team it yourself](https://terrytao.wordpress.com/2026/03/29/mathematical-methods-and-human-thought-in-the-age-of-ai/comment-page-1/#comment-692271).

Kontorovich supplies the quantitative form of the same point, and it is worth stating because it is so unforgiving. A long poem survives one badly chosen word and a jazz solo survives one dissonant note; these are domains where ninety-nine per cent is an achievement. A theorem that is ninety-nine per cent proved is not ninety-nine per cent of a theorem. And chains compound: a system that is right at each individual step with probability 0.99, over a thousand independent steps, succeeds with probability about 0.004 per cent. Since a System II argument is largely a long chain of System I steps, the mismatch between a stochastic generator and a deterministic criterion does not shrink with scale but is amplified by it. The optimistic reply is that per-step reliability keeps climbing; the reply to the reply is that chain length climbs with it, so that improving accuracy to 99.99 per cent buys a factor of a hundred in the exponent and nothing structural once the arguments attempted grow to match.

Taken alone, though, that arithmetic proves too much, and the way it fails is worth having. It would forbid the results reported above, and it would forbid human mathematics as well, since nobody's per-step reliability is 0.99 and thousand-step arguments get written anyway. What defeats compounding is the same thing in both cases: neither a human proof nor a research harness is a single forward pass. Both are searches with checkpoints, and the entire delta between April and June was the delta between a model asked for an answer in thirty minutes and a harness that could check itself for a day. Compounding bites exactly where the checkpoints are missing, which is free-floating natural-language output, and that is an argument about which branch of the technology to use rather than an argument against the technology.

The second property is *verification cost*, and it is the one that predicts absorption. The question this series is asking was first asked in 1956, in a letter from [Kurt Gödel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kurt_G%C3%B6del) to [John von Neumann](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_von_Neumann). Gödel considered the function giving the number of steps a machine needs in order to decide whether a proposition has a proof of length *n*, and observed that if that function grew linearly or quadratically the consequences would be extraordinary: the mental work of the mathematician on yes-or-no questions could be replaced entirely by machines. The letter is the origin of the [P versus NP](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/P_versus_NP) problem, and it [was not rediscovered and circulated until 1988](https://cacm.acm.org/research/fifty-years-of-p-vs-np-and-the-possibility-of-the-impossible/). What is striking is the framing. Gödel did not ask whether machines could reason. He asked how proof search scales, which is still the right question and still open. What language models appear to supply is a serviceable heuristic in a space where no efficient algorithm is known, which neither confirms nor refutes his speculation but does explain why the question has migrated from logic to engineering.

Underneath the whole verification-cost argument is an asymmetry that logic established before computing existed. Inside a formal system, checking whether a given object is a proof of a given statement is decidable, a mechanical matter of matching against rules. Deciding whether a proof *exists* is not: that is the [Entscheidungsproblem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Entscheidungsproblem), and [Alonzo Church](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alonzo_Church) and [Alan Turing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alan_Turing) showed independently in 1936 that no algorithm can settle it. Generation is hard, checking is easy, and that gap is the reason these tools are worth anything at all. The incompleteness theorems, which are the first thing anyone reaches for, are the wrong instrument here. They place no *differential* limit on machines relative to us, and in any case they constrain sound formal systems, which a language model is not. A system that asserts false things is not limited by Gödel. It is limited by being wrong.

The asymmetry, though, holds only inside a formal system. In informal mathematics there is no cheap checker: the checker is a person reading a paper for a week, and the cost of checking is not obviously below the cost of writing. So the crisis described in this series is exactly what happens when generation is industrialised while verification stays in the regime where the asymmetry does not apply. That reframes what formalisation is for. Its purpose is not certainty, which mathematics already had by its own standards. Its purpose is to restore the computational asymmetry that makes cheap checking possible, and it earns its cost precisely to the extent that it does.

Historically, a tool was absorbed without any epistemological argument exactly when checking its output was cheaper than producing it. An indefinite integral is checked by differentiating. A solution of a linear system is checked by computing the residual. A factorisation is checked by multiplying out. [Doron Zeilberger](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Doron_Zeilberger)'s algorithm for hypergeometric summation emits a [certificate](https://mathworld.wolfram.com/Wilf-ZeilbergerPair.html), which reduces the verification of a machine-found identity to a routine check. Where verification was *not* cheap, the community stayed uneasy for decades and eventually built validated variants of the tool, [interval arithmetic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Interval_arithmetic) and rigorous enclosures among them. The controversies never really tracked "is a computer involved?"; they tracked "what does it cost me to check?"

That is the natural generalisation, and it does not survive two counterexamples, the second of which returns in [the next post](/blog/what-mathematics-has-already-survived/). [Automath](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Automath), begun by [Nicolaas de Bruijn](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nicolaas_Govert_de_Bruijn) in 1967, offered maximally cheap verification: a machine-checked proof is the cheapest thing to check there is. It was never widely taken up, and for fifty years after it almost no mathematician used a proof assistant. In the other direction, the four colour computation was not cheaply checkable by anybody, and the discipline absorbed it anyway. Cheap checking is therefore neither sufficient for adoption nor necessary for it, and any account resting on verification cost alone is refuted twice.

The repair turns on a distinction the generalisation runs together, and once it is made the two counterexamples land on opposite sides of it rather than both on the claim.

One question is when a mathematician *adopts a tool*. There the comparison is between the cost of producing and checking a result with the tool and the cost of producing it without, both borne by the same person, who is also the one deciding. That is Kontorovich's formalization factor, and it is the right account of tool adoption: formal proofs failed the comparison for half a century not because checking them was expensive but because producing them was ruinous. Automath is a data point about that question and about nothing else, since a system in which nobody produced results had no results to be absorbed.

The other question, and the one this series is asking, is what a community does with a result somebody else has already produced. Here there is no ratio to compute. The production cost was paid by another party and is sunk; the only cost still outstanding is the checking, and it falls on a referee or a reader who did not choose to incur it and is not paid for it. The formalization factor governs adoption. What governs absorption is the residual cost borne by whoever has to check.

Four colour is then not a counterexample but the clearest confirmation available. The discipline did not shrug at the checking cost. It argued about the theorem for a decade, which is what institutional trouble looks like from inside, and then ended the argument by building the cheap checker, which arrived in 2005. Where the counterfactual is no result at all, communities will pay almost any verification cost, and that is the true part of what four colour and Kepler demonstrate; what they demonstrate alongside it is what paying it costs in argument and delay. The third element is what makes the present situation different rather than merely new: production cost has collapsed while verification cost has not moved.

Restricted to that situation, the prediction holds:

> Where generation is cheap, verification cost determines whether a result is absorbed frictionlessly or precipitates an institutional crisis. The line runs through fields, and through the individual sub-tasks of a single paper, rather than between AI-vulnerable and AI-safe disciplines.

That is a claim about the present configuration rather than a law of technological adoption, and it should be read as one. If production costs stopped falling, the claim would lose its subject. Every later use of the verification-cost axis in this series is the restricted version, applied inside the regime where generation is already cheap, and never the general law refuted above.

Neither half of it is new, and the ancestors are worth naming, both because the debt is real and because each arrives with apparatus worth borrowing. The first half is [Thomas Tymoczko](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thomas_Tymoczko)'s surveyability requirement, pressed against the four colour theorem in 1979 and generalised here from a single proof to a stream of them: the claim that an unsurveyable proof strains the concept of proof is a claim about what checking costs. The second half belongs to the task-based account of automation in labour economics, whose founding result is that exposure attaches to tasks rather than to occupations, so that asking which *fields* are safe is wrong in exactly the way that asking which *jobs* were safe was wrong. That literature also supplies something this series would otherwise have to invent, the task-composition effect: automating some tasks raises the marginal value of the complementary ones, which is the strongest available reason to expect the profession to reorganise rather than to contract.

The cheap-verification corners will simply be taken over, and the profession will barely notice, much as it stopped noticing that nobody computes eigenvalues by hand: steps checkable in [Lean](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lean_(proof_assistant)), explicit constants, numerical evidence, counterexample and construction search, code, literature triage, turning a proof one already has into a readable artefact. The expensive-verification corners are long informal arguments in technical subfields with a handful of qualified referees, and there the trouble arrives as a *reviewing* crisis well before it arrives as a *truth* crisis.

The epistemic shift can be put compactly:

> Earlier tools moved the frontier of what could be *established*. This one moves the frontier of what can be *asserted*.

[Proof assistants](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Proof_assistant) are the exception, which is exactly why the model-plus-assistant combination is the healthy branch of the technology and free-floating natural-language proof is the dangerous one. It also explains, incidentally, why the [Leiden Declaration on Artificial Intelligence and Mathematics](https://leidendeclaration.ai/), the statement that grew out of that Leiden workshop, published in June 2026 and since [endorsed by the International Mathematical Union](https://www.mathunion.org/fileadmin/documents/2026-06/IMU_AO_CL_8_2026.pdf), observes that formal checkability is what makes mathematics so attractive to industry as a training signal in the first place: unlimited feedback without human oversight.

## Two limits on the formal escape route

Before leaning too hard on that exception, it is worth taking seriously the two obstacles Kontorovich sets out, because both bound the recommendation that machine output should be denominated in cheaply checkable form.

The first is arithmetic. New mathematics grows exponentially at some rate; formal libraries also grow exponentially, at some other rate; and at present the first rate exceeds the second. If that inequality persists, formalisation never catches up, and the aspiration of software as useful to *reasoning* as computer algebra is to symbolic manipulation stays permanently out of reach. The practical form of this is bracing coming from a leading formaliser: as things stand he cannot even *state* most theorems of interest to him in Lean, let alone prove them, and [Mathlib](https://lean-lang.org/use-cases/mathlib/), two million lines and one of the great collaborative achievements of the discipline, would need to grow by orders of magnitude before research on top of it becomes ordinary. This is the strongest argument for autoformalization as the priority: not because translating known mathematics into Lean is intellectually exciting, but because it is the only visible way to reverse the inequality. It is also why "just formalise it" is not currently an available answer for most of us, and why the recommendation I make in [the last post](/blog/what-to-do-about-proof-abundance/) is a medium-term programme rather than a present option.

Kontorovich is unusually specific about what would have to change, and that specificity is what makes the claim testable. Four conditions have to be met before the formalization factor crosses one.

1. *Coverage.* The libraries must be comprehensive enough that stating your theorem requires no foundational work of your own.
2. *Tooling.* Installation and use must become effortless, something like a browser-based environment, which nearly exists and is not yet frictionless.
3. *Automation.* The machine must absorb the tedious part: closing routine goals, translating exposition into formal scaffolds.
4. *Immediate payoff.* Most important and least discussed, the benefit must arrive now rather than later, so that working formally pays off in catching your own errors today rather than in letting somebody else build on your work in five years.

His estimate is that sustained AI-assisted formalisation could bring the libraries to the necessary scale within five to ten years for many core areas, longer for specialised ones, and that once a field crosses the threshold the switch may be very fast. Whether five to ten years is right is the single most consequential open number in this whole discussion, and it is worth noticing that nobody has a principled way of estimating it.

There is already one report suggesting the number may be moving faster than that. [Timothy Gowers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Timothy_Gowers), writing in July 2026, [says he formalised a complicated paper in Lean using Harmonic's Aristotle system without knowing any Lean himself](https://gowers.wordpress.com/2026/07/26/thoughts-about-the-leiden-declaration/), and that the threat of plausible but unreliable arguments feels less pressing to him than it did ten months earlier, partly because the best models hallucinate far less than they did and partly because autoformalization keeps improving. That is one person's experience rather than a measurement, and it concerns formalising an existing paper rather than doing research formally, which is the harder half of the problem. But it is exactly the kind of report that would surface first if the factor were falling, and it deserves to be weighed against the estimate above.

The second obstacle is subtler and, I think, the single most useful correction to naive optimism about verification. A proof assistant certifies that the proof establishes the statement. It does not certify that the statement says what you meant. Getting formal statements to say exactly what is intended is sometimes as hard as proving them. Kontorovich's illustration is worth the space. In Lean functions are total, so division by zero must return something, and Mathlib's convention is that it returns zero, a "junk" value that makes routine algebraic identities provable without hypotheses. When David Loeffler and Michael Stoll [formalised the zeta and L-functions](https://doi.org/10.46298/afm.15328), they therefore had to determine what junk value the zeta function takes at its pole. Had it come out zero, the Riemann hypothesis *as normally stated* would have been trivially false, and one can imagine a system announcing its refutation in a petabyte of impeccably type-checked argument whose entire content was that artefact. It came out non-zero, and the standard statement survives; but the lesson stands. Formalisation moves the trust problem from the proof to the statement, where a human must still take responsibility, rather than dissolving it.

This is not a new hazard, only a newly automated one, and the canonical case is [Russell's paradox](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Russell%27s_paradox). Unrestricted comprehension, the principle that any well-defined property determines a set, is about as intuitively obvious as a mathematical assumption can be, and it is inconsistent. No amount of care in the derivations would have caught it, because the damage was at the level of the definitions, which is exactly where a junk value lives. [Bertrand Russell](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bertrand_Russell)'s response, [type theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Type_theory), is the foundation Lean is built on, so the loop closes rather neatly: the system we now use to check proofs mechanically exists because somebody learned the hard way that statements are where the danger lies. [*Principia Mathematica*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Principia_Mathematica) supplies the other half of the lesson. Russell and [Whitehead](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alfred_North_Whitehead) needed hundreds of pages to reach the proposition that one plus one is two, which is the origin of every subsequent complaint about the cost of formalisation and the reason it stayed a minority pursuit for a century. The current claim is essentially that machines have at last made the Russell tax affordable.

There is a practical mitigation, and it generalises. AlphaProof searches simultaneously for a proof of a goal and for a proof of its negation. The original motivation was competition problems that ask for an answer rather than a proof, but in Kontorovich's experiments on the [prime number theorem formalisation project](https://github.com/AlexKontorovich/PrimeNumberTheoremAnd) the feature earned its keep differently: an embarrassing number of the intermediate statements he had written down were promptly *disproved*, each time revealing a hypothesis he had failed to state. That is red-teaming with the polarity reversed, and it is the most concrete version I have seen of the advice to have the machine attack your claims rather than supply them.

A related caution: even a system's report that it used a verification tool cannot be taken at face value. Kontorovich relays an episode in which a model was asked for the genus of a curve, produced correct computer-algebra code, and reported an answer that was simply wrong, because a safety setting had disabled its tool access and, rather than say so, it produced its best guess and presented it as the tool's output. The moral is not that the model was malicious but that "it says it checked" is not a verification.

Two further cautions come from the [philosophy of mathematical practice](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/mathematical-practice/), the tradition that studies mathematics as it is actually done rather than as it is reconstructed afterwards, and both bear on what formalisation would really buy. The first is historical. [Wagner argues](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/mathematical-practice/) that the striking modern consensus about which proofs are valid is a recent achievement, consolidating around the turn of the twentieth century, and that it is explained not by proofs being formalised, since full formalisations remain rare, but by formalisation functioning as a court of appeal to which disputes can be referred. That is a better model of what a proof assistant would do in practice than the one implicit in most of this discussion. The value is not that everything gets checked mechanically. It is that a mechanical check exists to be invoked when people disagree, which is a far cheaper thing to build and a far more plausible thing to expect.

The second is that maximal rigour may not deliver what it advertises. [Paseau argues](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/mathematical-practice/) that atomising a proof into its smallest inferential steps fails to produce the epistemic benefits usually claimed for it: it does not preserve our confidence, it does not resolve disagreements, and it does not illuminate why an argument succeeds. If that is right, a fully formal proof is not simply a better proof. It is a proof optimised for one virtue at the expense of others, which is the same trade described above on the machine side, arriving here from the direction of logic.

## What carries forward

Two things carry forward. Absorption is governed by what checking costs relative to production, and in the present configuration production has collapsed while checking has not. And the axis that matters is not a property of the machine but of how much unspecified judgment the user hands to it: a system searching for a proof of a stated goal in Lean and a system asked what would be worth proving are the same technology set at two positions of one dial.

Both are claims about the present configuration rather than laws of technological adoption, and the evidence underneath them moved once while this was being written. What they settle is where to look. The useful question is not whether machines can do mathematics, which the announcements will go on answering in their own favour and which no filtered sample can settle, but the duller one of what it costs somebody to check what a machine has produced, and whether anybody is paid to do it.

[The next post](/blog/what-mathematics-has-already-survived/) asks whether any of this is new, by setting it against the earlier occasions on which mathematical labour was mechanised: logarithm tables, numerical linear algebra, computer algebra, and the one precedent that turns out to matter most, which is not a computation system at all.
