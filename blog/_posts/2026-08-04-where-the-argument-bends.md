A framework that only fits the cases it was built from is worth little, and the framework this series has been building was assembled almost entirely from pure mathematics.

Two properties of the automated task do the work in it, and both were set out in [Generation is hard, checking is cheap](/blog/generation-is-hard-checking-is-cheap/). The first is *specification*: earlier mathematical instruments attacked tasks with a complete formal criterion of success, so their failure modes were systematic and could be taught, whereas a language model asked what to prove or what level of generality is right is working with no criterion at all, and its errors are consequently plausible rather than artefactual. The second is *verification cost*, and it is the one that predicts absorption. Where generation is cheap, what a result costs to check decides whether it is absorbed frictionlessly or precipitates an institutional crisis, and the line runs through the individual sub-tasks of a single paper rather than between AI-vulnerable and AI-safe disciplines. [When the proxies stop measuring](/blog/when-the-proxies-stop-measuring/) added the institutional half of the diagnosis, that the scarce factor moves to verification and digestion, and that the proxies by which mathematicians assess one another stop carrying information.

This post points that framework at two places it was not built from. The first is applied and computational mathematics, where success is measured by predictive adequacy rather than by deductive correctness, and where the axis consequently behaves in a way it was not designed for. The second is dimension theory, the subfield I work in, where it yields a hypothesis specific enough to be tested and to be wrong, together with a description of the experiment that would refute it. A prediction about somebody else's field costs nothing to make, which is why the second exercise is the one that matters.

## The applied and computational side

Everything so far in this series is about proof, which is to say about one corner of the subject. The verification-cost axis makes a claim about the rest, and the claim is worth testing, because it half fails, and the way it fails is more informative than another confirmation would be.

In applied and computational mathematics the criterion of success is not deductive correctness but predictive adequacy, and verification is correspondingly empirical: a held-out dataset, a convergence study, a comparison against a reference solution or against an experiment. That kind of checking is cheap, automatic, and already statistical in character. So the axis predicts, correctly, that this side of the discipline should have absorbed machine learning years earlier and with far less anguish than the proof-centred side is now managing: learned surrogates for expensive simulations, data-driven reduced-order models, symbolic regression for constitutive relations, neural closures. It did, and nobody wrote a declaration about it. The reason is not that applied mathematicians are less scrupulous. It is that they had a cheap acceptance test and pure mathematicians did not.

But two things change with the regime. The first is that the specification problem moves rather than disappearing. In pure mathematics the danger is a proof that establishes something other than what you meant, which is the [junk-value problem](/blog/generation-is-hard-checking-is-cheap/) in general form. In applied mathematics it is a model validated on one distribution and deployed on another. Both are failures of the statement rather than of the derivation, both are invisible to the checking apparatus, and both put the responsibility back on a human who has to know what question was actually asked. That the same failure appears in both regimes, wearing different clothes, is decent evidence that it is structural rather than incidental.

The second is that the crisis has a different shape. When verification is expensive and deductive, an excess of plausible material produces an *attention* problem: too much to check, and the cheap heuristics for deciding what to check carefully stop working. When verification is cheap and statistical, the same excess produces a *replication* problem: results that pass their own acceptance test and do not survive contact with anyone else's. Computational science has been living with the second for a decade and has developed treatments for it, including shared benchmarks, mandatory code and data release, preregistered validation protocols, and an established sociology of the reproducibility failure. Pure mathematics is heading for the first, and has almost none of the corresponding machinery, because until now it never needed any. The split is tidier than the reality, though. Unchecked lemmas propagating into later work is already a replication problem wearing different clothes, and the two pathologies are better read as ends of a spectrum than as alternatives.

So the axis survives, with an amendment: it predicts *which* pathology a field gets rather than whether it gets one. That amendment is useful in a practical way, because it identifies who has already solved the adjacent problem. On refereeing and attention allocation, mathematics should be watching machine learning. On validation protocols and the discipline of releasing what you actually ran, it should be watching computational science, which has been arguing about this since before the current wave. A last concession is owed here too. The historical comparisons of [the second post in this series](/blog/what-mathematics-has-already-survived/) are drawn entirely from pure mathematics, and the applied tradition has a quite different lineage, having been computational since the earliest electronic machines and never having had a purity to lose. Applied mathematicians reading the anxieties above will find some of them provincial. They are not wrong.

There is a further asymmetry worth naming, because it explains why the same events read as catastrophe in one part of the building and as liberation in another. For a great many researchers, mathematics is an *input* rather than an output: a cost paid on the way to understanding something else. Economists writing theory sections, physicists deriving what they need to model a system, engineers reaching for an estimate, all of them treat the technical difficulty as a toll rather than as the object. For those people the collapse in the cost of mathematics is straightforwardly good, and the more capable these systems become the better their working lives get. The distress is concentrated almost entirely among those for whom the mathematics is the output. That is a small population, it is the one this series is written from and about, and it should be honest that its perspective is not the general one.

## A view from fractal geometry

The two axes above give a definite prediction for dimension theory, and it is better stated as a hypothesis that could be false than as an extrapolation that cannot. The version I have heard from inside the field, and which I take as the thing to be tested, is that the tools should be good at construction, meaning the finding of examples, and at covering arguments of the sort that settle Assouad-type questions, and bad at measure-theoretic arguments: Hausdorff dimension, entropy methods, projections and slicings.

The constructive half of that is, as it happens, the best-evidenced claim about AI in mathematics anywhere. Reinforcement-learning systems have been producing counterexamples in combinatorics for several years; [FunSearch found cap-set constructions](https://arxiv.org/html/2601.13209v1) beating the previous records, and AlphaEvolve generalised the method from single functions to whole programs optimising several objectives at once. The most striking instance sits outside dimension theory but is structurally identical: in May 2026 an OpenAI model [disproved Erdős's unit-distance conjecture](https://openai.com/index/model-disproves-discrete-geometry-conjecture/) by constructing, with tools from algebraic number theory, point configurations that beat the square grid, a result [Timothy Gowers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Timothy_Gowers) said he [would have recommended for the *Annals* without hesitation](https://theconversation.com/an-ai-solution-to-an-80-year-old-problem-has-shocked-mathematicians-283686) had a human produced it. What made it possible is a mechanism rather than a mystery, and worth attending to. Most experts had spent their effort trying to prove the conjecture, and the few who hunted for counterexamples were unlikely to pursue a tedious high-dimensional construction with no encouraging sign along the way; as Jacob Tsimerman [put it](https://www.scientificamerican.com/article/ai-just-solved-an-80-year-old-erdos-problem-and-mathematicians-are-amazed/), the machines can play for longer and in more treacherous waters without getting overwhelmed. Patience in an unpromising search space, with a cheap check at the end, is exactly the shape of the constructive problems in this field.

So for the parameterised families, [Bedford–McMullen carpets](https://research-repository.st-andrews.ac.uk/bitstream/handle/10023/25230/BMsurvey.pdf?sequence=1) and their Lalley–Gatzouras and Barański generalisations, self-affine systems, iterated function systems with prescribed overlaps, and for questions of the form *does there exist a set realising this configuration of dimensions?*, the prediction rests on real evidence. Expect extremal and counterexample constructions before theorems: candidate systems whose [Assouad spectrum](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2005.03763) has a prescribed shape, parameter regions where a dimension formula fails, boundary cases located numerically and then proved by hand. The same goes for transfer-operator and pressure computations, for verifying separation conditions in explicit families, and for the routine covering estimates that fill the middle third of many papers.

The negative half is a different kind of claim, and at present it cannot be checked, not because it is wrong but because nothing in the public record bears on it. There is no benchmark stratified by argument type, no [First Proof](https://1stproof.org/) problem in geometric measure theory, and essentially no reported attempts in the subfield at all. What exists instead is a literature on generic failure modes, and it is suggestive without being specific: models [cycle through variations of a failed approach](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2512.19287) rather than recognising that the strategy itself must be abandoned, they report identical confidence for correct and incorrect claims, and failure rates rise [with the length of the input](https://arxiv.org/html/2604.07593v2) and, by the compounding argument, with the length of the chain. Those are precisely the conditions under which a Frostman-type lower bound or an entropy-increase estimate goes wrong. But they are generic conditions. They predict failure on any long estimate chain in any field, which supports the mechanism without supporting the subfield-specific claim.

The dichotomy also cross-cuts itself in two places, and repairing it produces something sharper. Hausdorff dimension *upper* bounds are covering arguments: exhibit an efficient cover and you are finished. Assouad dimension *upper* bounds are quantified over all scales and all locations, with no witness to produce at the end. So the line does not run between Hausdorff and Assouad. What separates the two regimes is whether the argument terminates in a *witness* (a cover, a set, a measure, a parameter) or in a statement quantified over everything, with no object to hand the checker. The good cases are the existential ones and the hard cases are the universal ones; the measure-theoretic machinery looks hard because in this field it is where the universal statements happen to live.

That distinction is not a new invention, and naming its ancestry sharpens it further. The witness-versus-universal split is close to the constructive-versus-classical one. Under the [Brouwer–Heyting–Kolmogorov reading](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brouwer%E2%80%93Heyting%E2%80%93Kolmogorov_interpretation) of the logical connectives, a proof of an existential statement simply *is* a witness together with a verification that it works, and the propositions-as-types tradition makes that literal: a proof with constructive content is a program, which is to say an object a machine can produce and a checker can run again. [L. E. J. Brouwer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/L._E._J._Brouwer) proposed [intuitionism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Intuitionism) with none of this in view, and was hostile to formalisation of every kind, but the line he insisted on turns out to track the line that matters here. The hypothesis can therefore be stated more sharply than it was at the start: the machine-friendly fragment of a field may be approximately its *constructive* fragment, because constructive content is exactly the content that arrives with a checkable object attached.

Stated that way it also predicts its own exceptions, which is the mark of a better formulation. Exhibiting a measure and checking a [Frostman condition](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2005.03763) is itself a witness argument, so the constructive line does not coincide with the measure-theoretic one, and parts of Hausdorff dimension theory should sit on the friendly side after all. What sits on the far side is the *existence* half of [Frostman's lemma](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2005.03763), the almost-every-direction statements of projection theory, and anything else where the conclusion is asserted without any object being produced. That is a more discriminating prediction than "bad at measure theory", and it is wrong in ways somebody could actually notice.

A second and more local version of the same repair: the tractable arguments are those that reduce to a finite combinatorial model. Self-similar and self-affine systems with adequate separation reduce to symbolic dynamics, after which everything is finite data and optimisation over it, which is why the explicit carpet families have been computed so thoroughly, and why [Mackay's formula for the Assouad dimension of self-affine carpets](https://people.maths.bris.ac.uk/~jm13806/pdf/assouad-dim-carpet.pdf) is the kind of statement a search could have conjectured even though its proof is not. Overlaps destroy the finite model, and that is exactly where the entropy machinery had to be invented. If this is the right axis then the obstacle is not the measure theory as such but the loss of a finite model, and the hypothesis acquires a testable consequence: any question that recovers a finite model should become tractable, *including* questions about Hausdorff dimension.

The confound that makes all of this hard to test is that within geometric measure theory the entropy and projection arguments are also the ones humans find hardest. Three independent mechanisms push toward the observation the hypothesis predicts, which is why no amount of personal experience will settle it:

- *Difficulty.* An observed pattern of machine success on covering problems and failure on entropy problems is fully consistent with a difficulty-based explanation carrying no subfield content whatever.
- *Selection.* One hands the machine the problems one can check, which are the constructive ones, so the attempts are not a random sample either.
- *Reporting.* What becomes visible is then filtered down to the successes.

What would settle it is a stratified test, and it is small enough that someone in the field could run it. Take twenty or thirty lemmas from the recent literature with known human proofs, ideally unpublished ones out of people's drawers, so that training contamination is not a confounder as well. The selection criteria matter more than the source: a known complete human proof of a few pages, no published statement anywhere, and a roughly even split between lemmas whose proofs terminate in an exhibited object and lemmas whose proofs do not. Classify each in advance along two axes, argument type and human difficulty, with the difficulty ratings supplied by two or three working researchers in the area who cannot see the type classification and have not been told what the experiment is for. Run them blind under a fixed compute budget and have the outputs refereed. That is the design of First Proof, with its unpublished problems, controlled conditions and anonymous human refereeing, narrowed to one subfield, and it is precisely the community-owned benchmark the [Leiden group](https://leidendeclaration.ai/) recommended in the abstract. There is also a cheap private version: log every query with its classification and your predicted outcome *before* you see the result. Thirty entries over a year is a real signal, and nobody is currently collecting it.

Be exact about what that controls, though, because it is easy to overclaim for it. Logging before you see the result controls reporting bias completely. If you also record in advance which problems you would have been willing to hand over, it partly controls selection. It does nothing whatever about difficulty, which is the confound that matters most, and only the stratified design with blind ratings can touch that. A private log is therefore evidence about your own practice rather than about the machines.

A hypothesis that cannot describe its own refutation is not worth testing, so here is what a null result looks like. If, once difficulty is controlled, the systems succeed at roughly equal rates on witness-producing and universally quantified problems, then the constructive framing is wrong, the distinction drawn above is an artefact of which problems happen to be hard, and the operative variable is simply the length of the estimate chain and the compounding described in [the first post](/blog/generation-is-hard-checking-is-cheap/). That would be the duller result and the more useful one, since it would mean the same prediction applies to every subfield and nobody need run this test again.

If the hypothesis survives, the dangerous corners are the arguments on the universal side of that line, for the reason this series gives generally. Inverse theorems in additive combinatorics, entropy-increase methods, the projection and slicing apparatus are checkable by a small number of people worldwide, and the check is a chain of estimates rather than a structural insight one can spot-verify. A plausible but wrong AI-assisted argument in that style is close to the worst case: expensive to refute, easy to build on, and in a subfield where the referee pool is already the binding constraint. If the fractal geometry literature acquires load-bearing errors, that is where they will be.

The [August results](https://openai.com/index/ten-advances-in-mathematics/), ten problems open for decades settled with machine-checkable certificates attached, bear on this hypothesis from outside the field, and they bear on it favourably, which is worth saying since the hypothesis was formulated before them. Look at what actually fell. An explicit construction of a non-sofic group, a disproof of a rigidity conjecture and new bounds for sphere packing are a construction, a counterexample and a bound: three witness-producing results, each terminating in an object that can be handed to a checker, which is precisely the category predicted here to go first and precisely why Lean certificates could accompany them at all. One of the five named results does not fit, and it is the one worth dwelling on. A proof of Ehrhart's volume conjecture is a universally quantified statement with no object to hand anybody, which is the category predicted here to be hard, and it fell in the same batch. Counting only the three that fit would be the habit [the last post of this series](/blog/what-to-do-about-proof-abundance/) warns against, of redescribing every success as having been predicted all along, so let the score stand at three for and one against. That is not proof of the hypothesis in either direction, since the sample was selected for announcement and the selection may simply have favoured what could be certified. But if the constructive framing were wrong, one would expect the first crop to look considerably less lopsided than this, and it does not.

There is also a prediction about the shape of the field, and it is about shape rather than difficulty, since [the third post](/blog/when-the-proxies-stop-measuring/) gave the reasons for not expecting exposure to track any band of difficulty at all. Dimension theory holds a large stock of problems that are laborious rather than deep and that terminate in a computed object: compute a dimension for one more class of systems, extend a spectrum result to one more setting. If that stock thins, the centre of gravity moves toward the genuinely conceptual questions, exact overlaps and rigidity and the measure-theoretic foundations, and toward the digestion side: surveys, unified frameworks, and the canonicalisation of results currently scattered across a hundred preprints with incompatible notation. That is not a bad outcome; arguably the subject needs it. But there is a less comfortable corollary, and it is a claim about composition rather than about any band of difficulty. If constructions become cheap while structure theory does not, the incentive gradient tilts toward example-driven papers, because those are the ones that can now be produced quickly: a Goodhart effect operating inside a single subfield rather than across the discipline, and one that should be visible in the balance of the literature within a few years if it is happening at all.

## Where the framework stands

The verification-cost axis survives both tests, in each case with an amendment worth having. Outside pure proof it predicts *which* pathology a field gets rather than whether it gets one: an attention problem where verification is expensive and deductive, a replication problem where it is cheap and statistical. That amendment is practical rather than decorative, since it says who has already solved the adjacent problem. On refereeing and attention allocation, mathematics should be watching machine learning; on validation protocols and the discipline of releasing what was actually run, it should be watching computational science.

Inside dimension theory the axis sharpens into something better than the dichotomy it started from. What separates the tractable arguments from the hard ones is not the measure theory but whether the argument terminates in a witness that can be handed to a checker, which is close to the constructive-versus-classical distinction and predicts its own exceptions: exhibiting a measure and checking a Frostman condition belongs on the friendly side despite being thoroughly measure-theoretic, while the existence half of Frostman's lemma and the almost-every-direction statements of projection theory do not. The hypothesis also names the experiment that would kill it, and the confound that makes personal experience worthless as evidence for it.

It survived contact with the August results without having been designed for them, since a construction, a counterexample and a bound are exactly the shapes it says should fall first. That is encouraging rather than decisive, because a sample selected for announcement may simply have favoured what could be certified. What the framework has earned so far is the right to be tested properly, and the test is small enough that somebody in the field could run it this year.

What remains is what to do about any of it, which is the subject of [the last post in this series](/blog/what-to-do-about-proof-abundance/).
