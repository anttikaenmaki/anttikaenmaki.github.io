Almost every claim about AI and mathematics is an implicit historical analogy, and most of the analogies in circulation are wrong in both directions. The optimistic version says that this is computer algebra again and that the alarm will look silly in twenty years. The pessimistic version says that no earlier tool ever touched mathematical judgment, so no precedent applies at all. Both are testable against a record the discipline actually has.

The claim being tested comes from [Generation is hard, checking is cheap](/blog/generation-is-hard-checking-is-cheap/), the first post in this series: that where generation is cheap, verification cost decides whether a result is absorbed frictionlessly or precipitates an institutional crisis, and that the line runs through fields and through the individual sub-tasks of a single paper rather than between AI-vulnerable and AI-safe disciplines. That claim was stated there in a form already narrowed by two historical counterexamples, and a comparison worth invoking in passing is worth making properly.

This post tests it against the mechanisations the discipline has already absorbed, and against the episodes in which it could not check what it had been given: the four colour theorem, Kepler, the classification of finite simple groups, Perelman and Mochizuki. The verdict comes in two halves that point in opposite directions. Technologically the rhyme is closer than the sceptics allow, and the deskilling complaint was partly right every time. Institutionally the precedents are much further away than the optimists allow, and the distance has nothing to do with how clever the models are.

## Symbolic and numerical computation

Start with what genuinely rhymes, because it is more than the sceptics allow.

*The deskilling anxiety, and its partial vindication.* Every wave produced the same complaint, and the complaint was always partly right. Logarithm tables killed one skill and slide rules another. [Cleve Moler](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cleve_Moler) wrote [MATLAB](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/MATLAB) in the late 1970s as a front end to [LINPACK](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/LINPACK) and [EISPACK](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/EISPACK), so that his students would not have to write Fortran, and [commercialised it through MathWorks from 1984](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/MathWorks); a generation then stopped hand-rolling its [numerical linear algebra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Numerical_analysis). [Computer algebra systems](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Computer_algebra), from Macsyma through [Stephen Wolfram](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stephen_Wolfram)'s SMP to [Mathematica, released on 23 June 1988](https://computerhistory.org/profile/stephen-wolfram/), meant a generation stopped doing hard integrals by hand. Something real was lost each time: the tacit feel for conditioning, for the size of terms, for when an answer is implausible. The field survived because what was lost was, in [Terence Tao](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Terence_Tao)'s distinction, [*artificial* friction](https://mathstodon.xyz/@tao/116117404945094458), labour that supplied incidental mental exercise but was not itself the conceptual content. Whether the new tools also remove *natural* friction, the genuine conceptual difficulty that constitutes the learning, is the open question, and there the worry is both legitimate and largely without precedent.

*The legitimacy debate.* The [four colour theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Four_color_theorem), proved by [Kenneth Appel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kenneth_Appel) and [Wolfgang Haken](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wolfgang_Haken) in 1976, produced a decade of argument about whether a [computer-assisted proof](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Computer-assisted_proof) no human can survey is a proof at all, the sharpest statement being [Thomas Tymoczko](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thomas_Tymoczko)'s 1979 argument that computers were changing the nature of proof itself. The [Kepler conjecture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kepler_conjecture) produced the more extraordinary situation in which a panel of twelve referees, after four years, reported that it was ninety-nine per cent certain of [Thomas Hales](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thomas_Callister_Hales)' [proof by exhaustion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Proof_by_exhaustion) but could not certify the computations, which is not a thing referees are supposed to say. Both debates ended in the same place. The community changed its practices rather than its standards, and the once-controversial objects became routine: [Gonthier](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Georges_Gonthier) and Werner [formalised the four colour theorem in 2005](https://www.ams.org/notices/200811/tx081101382p.pdf), and Hales' own Flyspeck project completed a formal proof of Kepler in 2014.

*Discovery by machine as a legitimate mode.* Numerical experiment has been generating conjectures for sixty years and nobody now objects. Tao's enthusiasm for what he calls [population studies](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=zJvuaRVc8Bg), sweeping across thousands of problems at once instead of the intensive case study of one, is a continuation of that line rather than a break from it, and it is probably the clearest genuinely new capability on offer.

*Voluntary adoption, and what triggers it.* The precedent I had underweighted, and which [Alex Kontorovich](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alex_Kontorovich) puts at the centre of [his account of formalisation for the Congress's proceedings](https://arxiv.org/abs/2510.15924), is not a computation system at all but a typesetting one. [Donald Knuth](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Donald_Knuth) released [TeX](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/TeX) in 1978, when mathematicians still handed manuscripts to secretaries and waited months for something approximating their text back. Adoption was slow through the 1980s; then [Leslie Lamport](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leslie_Lamport)'s [LaTeX](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/LaTeX) supplied usable macros, and around 1990 the ratio of time-to-produce-a-paper-with-the-tool to time-to-produce-it-without dropped below one. Almost everybody then switched, with no coercion whatever, simply because it had become the faster way to work. Kontorovich calls this ratio the Knuth factor and proposes the obvious analogue: a *formalization factor*, the time to discover and formally verify a result divided by the time to discover and write it up in LaTeX. He puts it currently at somewhere between ten and a hundred depending on the subfield, and argues that when it crosses one the switch will be equally rapid and equally unforced. He also, rightly, retires the older [de Bruijn factor](https://www.cs.ru.nl/~freek/factor/factor.pdf), lines of formal code per line of prose, usually quoted at four to ten, on the grounds that lines of code stopped being a proxy for human effort the moment machines could generate them in bulk.

This is the producer-side counterpart of the claim about verification cost made in [the first post of this series](/blog/generation-is-hard-checking-is-cheap/), and the better-evidenced of the two, since it has a dated precedent behind it. The two answer different questions and should not be run together. The formalization factor governs whether a mathematician adopts a tool, weighing a cost they bear themselves and a decision that is theirs to make; the verification-cost axis governs what a community does with a result whose production cost somebody else has already paid, where the only outstanding cost falls on a checker who chose none of it. Kontorovich's ratio also carries a second observation I would not have thought to make. LaTeX was adopted not merely as a way of producing final documents but as an instrument of the research process itself: a living document in which lemmas can be inserted in logical order, dependencies cross-referenced, and the architecture of a proof reorganised as understanding changes. Typesetting became part of mathematical thinking rather than its final packaging. If formal systems follow that path, the trigger for adopting them will not be verification at all. It will be that working formally turns out to be a better way to keep track of what you have actually established.

Now the differences, which are larger, and which I would put in ascending order of importance.

*Determinism.* A computer algebra system is a function: same input, same output, wrong in the same way each time, and its bugs are findable and reportable. A model is a distribution. One cannot file a bug report against a distribution, and one cannot build the professional folklore that made computer algebra safe to use.

*Where in the stack it bites.* Mathematica automated the layer *below* the mathematician. The judgment layer (what to compute, whether the answer is interesting, how to organise the argument) was untouched, and was in fact made *more* valuable by the removal of the drudgery around it. The new tools aim at the judgment layer itself. That is why the disquiet is qualitatively different from 1988, and it is not merely conservatism: the earlier tools were complements to expertise, and this one is at least partly a substitute for it. Complements raise the value of the human input; substitutes lower it. The profession is right to notice which one it is looking at.

*The commons as an input.* Wolfram Research did not train Mathematica on the literature. The new systems are built out of the published corpus and out of formal libraries such as [Mathlib](https://lean-lang.org/use-cases/mathlib/), which converts mathematical writing from an *output* of the profession into a *factor of production* for someone else's product. This is why the [Leiden Declaration on Artificial Intelligence and Mathematics](https://leidendeclaration.ai/), the statement that grew out of a 2025 Leiden workshop on mechanisation and mathematical research and has since been [endorsed by the International Mathematical Union](https://www.mathunion.org/fileadmin/documents/2026-06/IMU_AO_CL_8_2026.pdf), treats attribution and licensing as structural rather than as a matter of etiquette, recommending among other things that material not be used as training data without consent and that publishing agreements permit authors to opt out. It is also why the citation findings of the [First Proof](https://1stproof.org/) referees, who [reviewed machine solutions to ten unpublished research lemmas](https://arxiv.org/html/2606.18119v1) and flagged missing, wrong and hallucinated references throughout, should be read as a symptom rather than as a fixable bug.

The licensing remedy, though, deserves more scrutiny than it usually receives, and [Timothy Gowers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Timothy_Gowers) has [given it some](https://gowers.wordpress.com/2026/07/26/thoughts-about-the-leiden-declaration/). His objection has two parts. The proposal presupposes a world in which journal articles are the main channel of dissemination, whereas almost all mathematics now reaches its readers as an arXiv preprint, with the journal supplying a mark of prestige afterwards. Once a paper is on the internet one can hardly ask that it not be used as training data, so the recommendation bites only on the small remainder that never appears there. The deeper question is what right is actually being infringed. We do not object when human mathematicians read our papers in order to become better mathematicians; we are usually delighted that somebody thought them worth the attention. An objection to a machine doing the same must therefore rest on not wanting machines to become better at mathematics, which is a coherent position but a different one, needing its own grounds: a worry about the threat to mathematical practice, or about mathematical ability transferring to more dangerous kinds of reasoning. The political economy point stands, since the corpus really has become a factor of production for somebody else's product. The licensing remedy stands on considerably weaker ground than the diagnosis does.

*Mathematics as an instrument.* The very property that makes mathematics epistemically privileged (that output can be checked automatically) is what has made it the training gym and the shop window for general-purpose reasoning. There is no analogue of this in the computer algebra era. It means the *choice of problems* is now subject to an external optimisation pressure: what is measurable, demonstrable, and announceable. Tao's [lecture at the International Congress of Mathematicians](https://www.mathunion.org/icm/icm-2026) puts the sharp form of this in terms of [Goodhart's law](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Goodhart%27s_law), the familiar formulation "when a measure becomes a target, it ceases to be a good measure" being a later compression, [usually credited to Marilyn Strathern](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Goodhart%27s_law), of what [Charles Goodhart](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charles_Goodhart) wrote in 1975. The Leiden Declaration puts the political form: the risk that questions come to be prioritised for their amenability to automation rather than for their significance.

Gowers thinks this overstates the industry's leverage, and his reason is worth holding onto. If a company produces a great deal of mathematics that mathematicians do not find interesting, no amount of money will persuade them otherwise. What happens instead is that mathematicians observe that the system does X but not Y, and the company then feels challenged to do Y. On that account the community is setting the target rather than being set by it, which is the reverse of the Goodhart worry and would mean the discipline retains more agency than the threat list assumes. My own reading is that both hold at different levels. Individual mathematicians certainly keep their own judgement about what is interesting, while the aggregate direction of investment is still decided by what can be demonstrated to outsiders. But the observation does suggest that whatever power the community has is exercised through exactly the channel the *Notices* article recommends, which is telling developers what counts.

*Exogeneity and pace.* Symbolic and numerical computation diffused over decades, driven by mathematicians' own demand, constrained by mathematicians' budgets, and subject to a de facto community veto: we adopted them when we trusted them. This wave is exogenous, funded on a scale that dwarfs the discipline, and moves on product cycles rather than journal cycles. The veto is gone, because students, referees, competitors and funders adopt regardless. That, more than any capability claim, is what makes a document like the Leiden Declaration necessary. Norms are the only remaining lever.

## Precedents, one crisis at a time

It is more informative to match each threat to its historical precedent than to argue about the general analogy.

- *Proofs no human can survey.* The four colour theorem: thirty years from controversy to formalised routine.
- *A proof nobody can fully referee.* Kepler: sixteen years from announcement to formal verification, resolved because the author did the work himself.
- *Proof abundance and indigestion.* The [classification of finite simple groups](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Classification_of_finite_simple_groups), tens of thousands of pages across several hundred papers by about a hundred authors, [prematurely announced complete by Daniel Gorenstein in 1983](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Daniel_Gorenstein), its final quasithin gap closed only in 2004 by [Michael Aschbacher](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Michael_Aschbacher) and Stephen Smith in a twelve-hundred-page argument, with a second-generation revision project still running four decades on.
- *Digestion as the real bottleneck.* [Grigori Perelman](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Grigori_Perelman)'s preprints of 2002–03, which required three independent teams and several years of work to render into checkable form, and which produced credit disputes on the way. The [Clay Institute's own account](https://www.claymath.org/millennium/poincare-conjecture/) is a study in how slow acceptance is.
- *Outright failure of digestion.* [Inter-universal Teichmüller theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inter-universal_Teichm%C3%BCller_theory), [Shinichi Mochizuki](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shinichi_Mochizuki)'s claimed proof of the [abc conjecture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abc_conjecture), published in 2021 and still unaccepted, with [Peter Scholze](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peter_Scholze) and [Jakob Stix](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jakob_Stix) having [set out publicly where they believe the argument fails](https://www.math.uni-bonn.de/people/scholze/WhyABCisStillaConjecture.pdf). Fourteen years and counting.
- *A values debate about standards.* [Arthur Jaffe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arthur_Jaffe) and [Frank Quinn](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Frank_Quinn_(mathematician))'s 1993 essay on "theoretical mathematics" and the [remarkable set of replies](https://arxiv.org/pdf/math/9404229) it drew, culminating in [William Thurston](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_Thurston)'s [*On proof and progress in mathematics*](https://arxiv.org/pdf/math/9404236), the essay Tao reaches for in his lecture, which argues that the measure of mathematical success is whether the work enables people to think more clearly rather than whether it meets a production quota of theorems.
- *Distributed credit.* The [Polymath Project](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polymath_Project), begun on Gowers's blog in 2009, which invented its authorship conventions as it went.

There is one further precedent, and it is a precedent for the *exercise* rather than for any of the crises: an earlier attempt at exactly this kind of forecast, which can now be marked. In the GAFA *Visions in Mathematics* volume of 2000, Gowers contributed [an essay on rough structure and classification](https://doi.org/10.1007/978-3-0346-0422-2_4) whose second section asked whether mathematics would still exist in 2099, and staged an imagined dialogue between a mathematician and a computer two or three decades hence. The mathematician asks whether a statement is true; the machine returns a technically correct but useless answer; pressed, it suggests an approach, then produces examples, then notices that a set it has generated resembles a Cantor set and extracts a bound. Anyone who has spent an afternoon with a chatbot will recognise the texture immediately, and Gowers's own gloss, that the computer is being extremely useful without doing anything particularly clever, is a fair description of where the tools sit today. That a dated forecast of this kind landed roughly on schedule is worth weighing against the general worthlessness of predictions in this area. Gowers also attached a warning: that such a golden age of human–machine collaboration might be short, giving way to a stage in which only a handful of outstanding mathematicians could find proofs the machines could not, and eventually to one in which the mathematician's job is mainly to operate theorem-proving machinery. A valuable skill, he allowed, but not pure mathematics as we now understand it.

Three lessons come out of that list.

The first is that *none of these were about computers*. The classification, Perelman and Mochizuki are entirely human instances of the pathology the ICM lecture predicts AI will mass-produce: results generated faster than the community can verify, digest, accept and canonicalise. The strain was already there. AI is a load multiplier on a structure that was already creaking, which is a reason to expect it to fail at joints we can already name.

The second is that every one of these was resolved *after the fact*, by codifying a norm under pressure. The Leiden Declaration is unusual in attempting the codification *before* the crisis matures, though it was not the first attempt: the American Mathematical Society's Committee on the Profession published [a white paper on AI and the mathematical community](https://www.ams.org/about-us/2024_CoProf_white_paper.pdf) in January 2024, two years earlier, which already treated attribution for the maintenance of shared resources, and the question of what mathematicians owe the public conversation about AI, as open problems for the profession. The fact that the Declaration carries IMU endorsement, that the [IMU has flagged it for continuing debate at the Congress](https://www.mathunion.org/imu-news/archive/2026/imu-news-137-may-2026), and that it passed [three thousand signatories](https://leidendeclaration.ai/signatories) within eight weeks, suggests the instinct is widely shared. The model its drafters had in mind is not a mathematical one at all: the [*Notices* report on that workshop](https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.24914) proposes a living statement of principles established by wide consultation and points explicitly to the [1975 Asilomar conference on recombinant DNA](https://dx.doi.org/10.1038/255442a0), where a scientific community imposed a moratorium and then a framework on itself while the technology was still young. Asilomar is the standard case both for the possibility of pre-emptive self-governance and for its limits, since what it mainly bought was time and legitimacy rather than control. That is roughly the right expectation here too.

The third is that the resolution was always a change in practice, never in the standard of truth. Nobody decided that four colour was true by fiat; they built the means to check it. The current analogue is available and obvious: make cheaply checkable output the currency in which machine contributions are denominated.

Against which, three ways the analogy fails, since a diagnosis built on precedent should be explicit about where the precedent stops. In every case above the generator was a human being who could be asked what they meant, who could be held responsible, and whose output was bounded by the hours in their life. The volume was therefore self-limiting, and the community controlled the clock: Flyspeck took sixteen years because nothing forced it to take less. None of that holds now. Generation is stochastic and unaccountable, volume is bounded by compute budgets rather than by human stamina, and the pace is set outside the discipline by actors with their own reasons for moving fast. The precedents are good for identifying *which joints* will fail, which is why they are worth having. They are poor guides to *how long* the community will have to fix them, and every one of them resolved on a timescale the community will not get again.

## What the precedents are good for

The technological comparison, then, is closer than the rhetoric suggests, and the institutional comparison is much further away, for five reasons that have nothing to do with capability. The tool is a distribution rather than a function, so no folklore of its failure modes can accumulate as it did for computer algebra. It aims at the judgment layer rather than at the layer beneath it, which makes it at least partly a substitute for expertise where the earlier tools were complements. It is built out of the mathematical commons rather than sold into it. It treats mathematics as an instrument for something else, which puts the choice of problems under an external optimisation pressure that has no analogue in 1988. And it arrives from outside the discipline on a timescale over which the community holds no veto, because students, referees, competitors and funders will adopt regardless.

What the precedents are good for is naming the joints. Every crisis in the list above was a failure of verification, digestion, canonicalisation or credit under a load the community could not absorb, none of them had anything to do with computers, and each was resolved after the fact by codifying a norm rather than by changing the standard of truth. Those are the joints that will fail again, and the list is short enough to act on: the current analogue of building the means to check the four colour theorem is to make cheaply checkable output the currency in which machine contributions are denominated.

What the precedents cannot supply is a schedule. In every one of them the generator was a human being who could be asked what they meant, whose output was bounded by the hours in a life, and whose pace the community controlled; Flyspeck took sixteen years because nothing forced it to take less. None of that holds now. The precedents identify which joints will fail. They are poor guides to how long there will be to fix them.
