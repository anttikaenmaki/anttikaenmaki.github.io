Diagnosis without prescription is cheap, and prescription without a way of being wrong is cheaper still.

The diagnosis is the work of the four preceding posts in this series, and it comes to four claims. Where generation is cheap, machine output is absorbed without friction wherever checking is cheap and causes institutional trouble wherever it is not, which is a line running through the sub-tasks of a single paper rather than between disciplines ([Generation is hard, checking is cheap](/blog/generation-is-hard-checking-is-cheap/)). The historical precedents identify which joints will fail, and are poor guides to how long there will be to fix them ([What mathematics has already survived](/blog/what-mathematics-has-already-survived/)). The goals of mathematics were joint products of one scarce input, so cheap generation decouples them: the scarce factor moves to verification and digestion, which nobody is paid to do, and the proxies by which mathematicians assess one another stop carrying information ([When the proxies stop measuring](/blog/when-the-proxies-stop-measuring/)). And the framework predicts which pathology a field gets rather than whether it gets one, with the machine-friendly fragment of a subject looking approximately like its constructive fragment ([Where the argument bends](/blog/where-the-argument-bends/)).

None of that obliges anybody to do anything, which is the gap this post is meant to close. It sets out what an individual, an editorial board, a supervisor, a department and a funder might actually do, insists that each recommendation be enforceable without any ability to detect whether a tool was used, states which quantities would falsify the argument, and weighs the two accounts of the near future that the present evidence supports without discriminating between them. It closes with my own practice.

## Learning what the tools do

Before any question about how to use these systems comes a prior one about how anybody finds out what they can do, and it has no satisfactory answer at present. [The first post in this series](/blog/generation-is-hard-checking-is-cheap/) opened by conceding that no representative survey exists of what working mathematicians actually do with them. The practical consequence is that the information environment is not merely incomplete but skewed in a knowable direction.

Three distortions compound. Reporting bias runs against negative results, so what circulates is the successes. Developer announcements are the largest single source of public information about capability, and they are produced by the parties with the strongest interest in the answer. And the secondary literature amplifies: the position paper discussed in [the third post of this series](/blog/when-the-proxies-stop-measuring/) reached wide circulation in a form claiming that a laboratory had proved these systems can never make discoveries, which is neither what it argued nor who wrote it. Anyone forming an impression from what crosses their screen will be miscalibrated, and predictably so.

A further obstacle has nothing to do with distortion. A [survey of formal mathematical reasoning](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2412.16075) observes that the bottleneck for adopting proof assistants has been usability rather than capability, and calls for behavioural studies of mathematicians working with formal tools under ordinary conditions. Nobody has run those studies, which means the discipline has no systematic knowledge of its own practice at exactly the moment it needs some. The [AMS Committee on the Profession](https://www.ams.org/about-us/2024_CoProf_white_paper.pdf) identified the demand two years ago, noting that mathematicians would want assistance in developing their understanding of these tools, and that assistance has not materialised in any organised form.

Three responses follow, none adequate alone. Calibration has to be personal, which generalises the logging protocol proposed [for the fractal-geometry test](/blog/where-the-argument-bends/) into ordinary practice: record what you asked, what you expected, and what you got, before you see the result. It is the only evidence about these tools that is not filtered through somebody else's incentives. Community benchmarks are an epistemic public good and not merely a defence against Goodhart's law, since [First Proof](https://1stproof.org/), the controlled benchmark whose ten unpublished research lemmas were attempted under a fixed compute budget and refereed anonymously by mathematicians, exists partly because nobody could otherwise tell what the systems could do, and its most useful output was arguably not the seven-in-ten success rate but the referees' findings about attribution and exposition, which no vendor would have reported. And a small, cheap third: departments running internal seminars on these tools should ask people to demonstrate failures rather than successes, since the successes are over-represented everywhere else and the failures are what carry information.

## Welcome, resist, absorb

The cases for optimism and for concern are forecasts. What a community needs is a decision, and the decision requires sorting disruptions by whether they are worth having, worth fighting, or beyond either.

The criterion is already here, in [Terence Tao](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Terence_Tao)'s distinction between [*artificial* friction](https://mathstodon.xyz/@tao/116117404945094458), which belongs to the medium, and natural friction, which belongs to the mathematics. Removing the first is pure gain. Removing the second removes what produced the judgment. Applied to the disruptions under discussion, the sorting comes out reasonably clean.

*Welcome.* The lifting of the language tax, discussed in the epilogue and plausibly the largest single inequity in mathematical publishing. Literature search, where much duplicated effort is a failure of retrieval rather than of ability. The tedious verified layer, which nobody wanted as a career. Construction and counterexample search. Trustless collaboration at scales human trust cannot reach. And the retirement of norms that exist only to ration a scarce good, including secrecy about work in progress and the anxiety about being scooped.

*Resist.* The outsourcing of a student's first encounter with a problem, which is where the diagnostic moment lives. Evaluation drifting toward reputation as the last surviving triage signal. Volume treated as a signal of anything. And the quiet acceptance of results nobody has checked, because checking is unrewarded.

*Absorb.* The pace, the exogeneity, and the absence of any community veto over adoption, since students, referees, competitors and funders will adopt regardless. This category is the one most often mistaken for the second, and the mistake is expensive: effort spent resisting what cannot be resisted is effort not spent on the middle category, which can be.

The sorting is not neutral and should not pretend to be. Anyone who believes the conceptual difficulty of a subject is inseparable from the labour of writing it up will move the first item from the first list to the second. The value of stating the criterion is that it makes such disagreements locatable rather than temperamental.

## A workable stance

Synthesising the practical advice, and adding what seems to me to follow from it.

Use the tools anywhere you can red-team the output yourself. Tao's test is the sharpest available: if you could not present the output in a seminar and answer questions on it without further assistance, it does not belong in your workflow. His [five conditions for letting an agent work essentially unsupervised](https://terrytao.wordpress.com/2026/07/16/two-more-apps-visualizing-the-zeta-process-and-the-motions-of-the-heavens/) are also worth internalising. The task is not mission-critical; the product is self-contained; it is deterministic and sandboxed; it is not replacing a primary skill; and it is not duplicating anyone else's effort.

Do not ask for the answer to a problem you cannot solve yourself. If you are stuck, invert the interaction: propose your own strategies and have the tool attack them.

There is a good theoretical reason why that inversion works. Catarina Dutilh Novaes [models proof as a semi-adversarial dialogue](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/mathematical-practice/) between a prover and a skeptic, in which the requirement that steps be truth-preserving comes from the need to convince an opponent, while the requirement that they be clear comes from the cooperative half of the exchange. On that account the properties we group under the name of rigour exist because authors have internalised a skeptical reader. It yields a one-line diagnosis of the failure mode discussed above: a language model has internalised a *plausibility* critic rather than a skeptical one, which is exactly why its errors are well formed. Supplying the skeptic yourself is therefore not a workaround. It is restoring the missing half of the dialogue.

Disclose, and make disclosure boring. The worst equilibrium is covert use plus public disapproval, which is where every stigmatised technology ends up, and which his [lecture at the International Congress of Mathematicians](https://www.mathunion.org/icm/icm-2026) names explicitly as the scenario to avoid. A short tool-and-resources section in every paper, including one that says "none", normalises it fastest. Both of the sources discussed here practise what they recommend: the lecture slides disclose that AI was used for text autocompletion and diagram generation, Tao's summary page discloses his industry entanglements, and that page was itself drafted with AI assistance and then corrected by him. The [Erdős problems site's policy](https://terrytao.wordpress.com/2025/12/08/the-story-of-erdos-problem-126/) is the cleanest existing model: contributions welcome provided the AI use is disclosed *and* the content has been independently checked by the contributor without further AI help.

Attribute proactively. Since the tools demonstrably fail at citation, the obligation shifts to the author; where you cannot establish where an idea came from, say so in print.

The [Leiden Declaration](https://leidendeclaration.ai/)'s own recommendation here is to affirm the humanity of authorship, on the grounds that credit and responsibility belong to humans within the mathematical community and that automation obscures rather than replaces the collective human labour behind a result. [Timothy Gowers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Timothy_Gowers) puts the hard case: what does that mean for the [unit-distance construction](https://openai.com/index/model-disproves-discrete-geometry-conjecture/), in which a model disproved a conjecture of Erdős by building point configurations that beat the square grid? Humans did a fine job of explaining the proof the model produced, and the model drew on highly non-trivial mathematics that humans had made, but the solution itself has not been credited to any human, and in his view should not be. A recommendation easy to endorse in the abstract and awkward to apply to the first real instance is usually doing something other than it appears to. My own reading is that it is best understood as a rule about *responsibility* rather than about credit. Somebody has to be answerable for a result being right, and that somebody cannot be a system that will not remember the claim tomorrow. Credit can float free. Accountability cannot.

Whether that obligation extends to formal statements is genuinely disputed, and the dispute is worth registering rather than resolving. [Alex Kontorovich](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alex_Kontorovich)'s position is that a mathematician must hold themselves personally responsible for any formal statement bearing their name, translated by hand or by machine, precisely because of the junk-value problem [set out in the first post of this series](/blog/generation-is-hard-checking-is-cheap/): a proof assistant certifies that the proof establishes the statement, not that the statement says what you meant. Christian Szegedy, whose position Kontorovich [reports and rejects](https://arxiv.org/abs/2510.15924), takes the opposite view: it does not much matter if formalised statements and definitions are initially wrong, because mathematics is robust. Definitions are not invented arbitrarily but refined over years, and the historical record supports him. Topology worked its way from intervals on the line, to balls in metric spaces, to open sets satisfying axioms, each refactoring improving the notion; neither Gauss's class number problem nor the Poincaré conjecture was first stated in the form we now use. Wrong statements that are useful get corrected through use, and wrong statements that are useless are harmless because nobody builds on them. My own reading is that the disagreement is really about timescale. Szegedy is describing an equilibrium property of the literature over decades, and he is probably right about it; Kontorovich is describing the position of a researcher inside the transient, where a mis-stated definition can cost you two years. Both can hold at once, and only one of them is your problem.

## Publishing and review

The editorial side is now your problem rather than someone else's. The Declaration's recommendation that professional bodies and journals lead on disclosure, rigour and authorship lands directly on editorial boards, and *Communications in Mathematics* will need a position sooner than it would like. Four decisions are worth making in advance: whether a tool-disclosure statement is required; whether formalisation may be requested for results whose verification would otherwise be infeasible; what the policy is on AI-assisted referee reports; and whether to adopt something like the rule of thumb the ICM lecture proposes, that authors who cannot convincingly give a clear, correct, properly attributed expert-level talk on their own result should not be publishing it. That last is severe, but it has the singular advantage of being enforceable without any ability to detect AI use, which is a property every workable policy will need.

[One concrete proposal](https://x.com/dmitryrybin1/status/2081241894919496002) now circulating in response to those slides deserves more attention than it has had, because it is the first suggestion I have seen that meets that condition and attacks the reviewing bottleneck at the same time. It is to restructure the mathematical paper itself:

1. Problem and background.
2. A summary of the solution for experts.
3. An explicit account of the new ideas introduced.
4. The consequences for other problems and for future work.
5. The full proof.
6. Formal verification where possible.

Review then gates on the first four sections, and a submission whose first four sections are empty is declined without anyone reading the proof. The merits are substantial. It restores a cheap filter to replace the one that AI destroys, since the opening sections take an expert minutes where checking a proof takes days. It makes exposition load-bearing rather than ornamental, which is exactly the reweighting the bottleneck argument calls for. And it largely formalises what good papers already do and what referees already do informally, which makes it the kind of reform that can actually pass.

It needs two repairs. The stated rationale, that the prose sections are easier to review and can even be reviewed with AI assistance, has the argument backwards, because fluent prose is precisely what these systems produce most readily, and screening them with fluent prose is Goodhart's law volunteering for duty. What is genuinely hard to fake is narrower: an accurate account of what is *new relative to the existing literature*, and of which other problems the result bears on. That is novelty and attribution judgement, which is where the systems demonstrably fail, as the hallucinated citations in the First Proof reports and the emphasis on prior-art checking in DeepMind's own case study both show. So the load-bearing sections are the third and fourth rather than the second, and a policy should say so explicitly. The second repair is that correctness cannot be declared irrelevant. Gating *review* on exposition is a change of practice; ignoring whether the proof is right is a change in the standard of truth, and the historical record of [the second post in this series](/blog/what-mathematics-has-already-survived/) suggests the first kind of reform succeeds while the second has never once been attempted.

The principle underneath the proposal already has a name, and a better statement than the proposal itself gives it. Kenny Easwaran argues that a proof should be *transferable*, meaning that a reader can reconstruct the reasoning from the text without having to rely on the author's authority, and Silvia De Toffoli makes the related demand that proofs be [*shareable*](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/mathematical-practice/). Transferability is precisely the property that machine-generated proofs fail, and it is a norm the discipline already had rather than one invented for the present emergency. A journal wanting a rule with a principle behind it should ask for transferability and treat the section structure as one means of securing it.

There is also a cost worth stating plainly rather than discovering later. A policy of this shape falls hardest on exactly the contributor that trustless verification was supposed to admit: someone with a correct proof, imperfect English, and an incomplete grasp of who else has worked on the problem. Decisive papers do arrive from unknown authors, and are recognised as decisive; a filter tuned to reject unsituated work will reject some of those too. That is the tension named in [the third post](/blog/when-the-proxies-stop-measuring/), between formal verification admitting an unknown contributor on the strength of the proof alone and volume pushing journals toward reputational gating, appearing again in concrete form. It is not resolvable, only choosable, and an editorial board adopting the proposal should know which way it is choosing.

One further editorial decision belongs on that list, and it follows more directly from the bottleneck argument than any of the others. If digestion is the scarce input, and if it is unpaid and invisible on a curriculum vitae, then somebody has to build a venue in which doing it counts. The cheap version is a category: a journal that accepts *digestion papers* as first-class submissions, reviewed on their own criteria, namely whether the account is correct, whether it is transferable in Easwaran's sense, and whether it leaves the area easier to enter than it found it. That is not a survey section renamed. A survey reports what was done, while a digestion paper takes a result whose proof exists and is not understood and produces the version that can be built upon. The more ambitious variant is registered exposition, in which a journal commissions the digest of a designated result in advance and commits to publishing it, converting the work from a favour into an assignment with a deadline and a line on a curriculum vitae. Both are imperfect, and Gowers's recursion applies to both. But the central recommendation of this series is empty without something of roughly this shape attached to it, and an imperfect mechanism named is worth more than a correct diagnosis left as an observation.

Refereeing needs norms of its own, and this series has so far diagnosed the problem without proposing any. Three seem to follow. Disclosure should be symmetric, so that a referee who used a tool on a report owes the editor the statement an author owes the journal, and for the same reason, which is that undisclosed use combined with public disapproval is the worst available equilibrium. A referee should be entitled to request a live exchange with the author, a talk or a session of questions, which is the enforceable form of the seminar test and far cheaper than verifying a long argument line by line. And a report should state what was checked and what was taken on trust, which referees have always known privately and which becomes load-bearing information once the proportion taken on trust begins to rise.

Evaluation needs the same treatment and has had even less. If output volume stops signalling anything, the answer cannot be a better count. It has to be reading. The concrete form is nominate and examine: a candidate for a post or a promotion nominates a small number of items, three perhaps, and the committee reads those properly and questions the candidate on them. It is expensive, and it is what committees claim to do already and mostly do not. It also has the property every workable norm needs, since it requires no ability to detect whether a tool was used. What it tests is whether the candidate owns the work in the only sense that matters.

## Teaching and supervision

The uncomfortable implication of the deskilling argument is that the friction you remove from a student is the training you deny them. Emphasising process and verification over answers, asking students to submit their prompts alongside their independent checks, and reserving unassisted work for the parts that actually bear weight is the best available compromise. There is no clean solution, and pretending otherwise is worse than admitting the trade-off.

But the pessimism there needs a counterweight, and Kontorovich supplies a good one. Imagine teaching chess entirely by reciting move sequences in algebraic notation, never showing the board. Aficionados update the position in their heads effortlessly; everyone else is lost. That, he observes, is precisely how we teach proof. At every line of an argument the board (the objects in play, the hypotheses available, the goal remaining) has changed, and the expert tracks it without noticing while the beginner reconstructs it laboriously and often wrongly from terse prose. In a proof assistant the board is simply displayed, continuously, and the student can look. He makes the same point historically: before about 1600 algebra was conducted in sentences, and the move to writing *x(x+a) = b* did not make the mathematics easier so much as make it visible. If that analogy holds, the technology that threatens to remove natural friction may also remove a great deal of the artificial kind, and the artificial kind has been doing real damage to beginners for a long time.

This suggests an operational test for a distinction that is otherwise hard to apply. Friction that arises from the *notation*, from what our conventions of exposition leave implicit, is artificial, and removing it is pure gain. Friction that arises from the *mathematics*, the difficulty of seeing which of several approaches will work, is natural, and removing it removes the training. Displaying the proof state is the first kind. Handing over the choice of strategy is the second. Most of the practical questions about students reduce to keeping the two apart.

This is not purely a matter of taste, because the learning-science literature has been studying it for decades under other names. [Retrieval practice](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16507066/) outperforms rereading for durable retention; the desirable-difficulties tradition holds that certain kinds of effort improve long-term learning precisely because they are effortful; and [productive failure](https://www.cse.iitk.ac.in/users/se367/14/Readings/papers/kapur-14_productive-failure-in-learning-math.pdf) suggests that attempting and failing before instruction improves later understanding, provided good feedback follows. What that body of work implies is unambiguous. A tool that removes the first failed attempt removes the diagnostic moment, and the danger is less that students cheat than that they acquire an accelerated superficiality: faster summaries, faster hints, faster apparent clarity, and a [concept image](https://wrap.warwick.ac.uk/id/eprint/507/) that never gets forced into alignment with the definition. The corresponding recommendation is narrow and cheap. The student's first encounter with a problem should not be outsourced, and the tool's best role in proof is as a critic of the student's argument rather than a source of one.

There is one curricular change on which the sources here converge independently, and it is the one I would push hardest. [Commelin and his co-authors](https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.24914), reporting the Leiden workshop for the *Notices*, recommend broadening curricula to emphasise posing problems, communicating ideas, and *critiquing purported logical arguments*; the educational literature identifies proof validation, judging whether a purported proof actually proves what it claims, as a skill undergraduates conspicuously lack. In a world where plausible arguments are cheap and unlimited, that is no longer a peripheral competence. It is the core one, and almost nobody teaches it explicitly.

At the level of what a department can actually decide next term, that resolves into four changes, none of which requires anyone to detect AI use.

1. *Grade the critique, not the solution.* Distribute arguments, some sound and some subtly broken, and mark the diagnosis.
2. *Restore oral examination* for a small number of assessments, since talking through your own proof under questioning is the one format that cannot be outsourced and the one that most closely resembles what the profession actually does.
3. *Collect the first attempt* as a submitted artefact in its own right, before any assistance, so that the diagnostic moment survives contact with the tool.
4. *Be honest about the cost* of the remaining unassisted work rather than pretending it is efficient. A course that keeps its hardest sequence deliberately manual is buying something real, and it should say what it is buying and why.

The common feature is that all four evaluate the process rather than the product, which is the only durable response once products are free.

Supporting junior researchers raises a question the teaching material does not reach, and the argument of [the third post](/blog/when-the-proxies-stop-measuring/) about where career value concentrates has sharpened it. If the value of a portfolio of solid papers falls, the people holding portfolios are precisely those without permanent positions. Two things follow for anyone advising them. Problems should be selected by shape rather than by difficulty, since exposure tracks whether work terminates in something checkable and how long its inference chains run, not how hard it is. And there is a matter of honesty: senior mathematicians are currently giving career advice about a profession they may not be able to describe in ten years, and saying so is better than projecting a confidence the evidence does not support.

There is also an argument for apprenticeship that comes from the machines' own deficiencies rather than from sentiment, and it is the strongest one available. In [interviews conducted by Epoch AI](https://epoch.ai/frontiermath/expert-perspectives), Tao observes that what these systems are worst at is adjusting after a failed approach, extracting from an attempt that did not work the lesson that reshapes the next one. Part of the reason is visible in the training data. We publish our best proofs of our best theorems, so the corpus holds the successes and almost none of the process, and the systems never see what a graduate student sees, which is somebody trying something foolish and being told why it was foolish. The capacity supervision exists to produce is therefore exactly the capacity the corpus omits. That is not grounds for complacency, since the gap could be closed deliberately. But it does mean the case for apprenticeship rests on something more durable than nostalgia.

## Institutional options

The recommendations above are addressed to individuals, and individuals cannot repair an incentive problem. What follows is a sketch of institutional options rather than a programme, set out with their costs, because each carries a real one and because which is worth adopting depends on which future arrives.

*Changing the unit of work.* At present the unit is the paper: a fixed artefact, published once, corrected by erratum if at all. The alternative is the maintained object, a versioned repository with a formal core, an expository layer and a dependency graph, of which papers are snapshots. Mathematics would be adopting a distinction software made long ago between a release and a repository. The attraction is that corrections propagate instead of accumulating, digestion acquires somewhere to live, and contributions become granular enough that a student can make a real one. The cost is not small: maintained objects need maintainers, the citation system assumes fixed artefacts, and establishing priority becomes harder rather than easier, which falls hardest on the people who most need a clear record. That is priority in the provenance sense distinguished in [the third post](/blog/when-the-proxies-stop-measuring/), the sense that survives and grows in importance as credit floats free, rather than the ranking convention listed above among the scarcity norms worth retiring.

*Two career tracks.* Discovery and canonicalisation, of equal standing, with promotion cases that treat a definitive treatment as equivalent to a theorem. Less radical than it sounds, since departments already know who their best expositors are and simply cannot pay them for it. The cost is that a second track tends to become a lower one, and the people steered into it are usually those with least power to refuse.

*Funding maintenance rather than only discovery.* Formal libraries resemble observatories or sequence databases more than they resemble projects, needing permanent staff and indefinite commitment, and grant cycles are badly shaped for that. The same argument covers benchmark infrastructure, which is the constructive answer to the Goodhart problem, since a community that will be measured anyway is better off owning the measure. The cost is that money spent on infrastructure is money not spent on people, and the discipline has never had to make that trade at scale.

*Replication.* If verification stops being free, mathematics needs what computational science already has: an expectation that you release what you actually ran, and that somebody occasionally checks. Cheap to state, expensive to staff.

One economic observation belongs alongside these, because it constrains how bad the employment picture can get. Academic mathematicians are, collectively, extremely cheap. [Noah Smith](https://www.noahpinion.blog/p/the-end-of-the-age-of-heroes)'s estimate for the total of all academic mathematics salaries in the United States is a few billion dollars, which is a rounding error against national output and comparable to what the country spends on minor consumer categories. Most of that pays for teaching rather than research, and the frontier research it does buy has never been justified by economic return, since outside cryptography and scattered physics applications most of it has no application at all. The profession is therefore already funded on the premise that human understanding of mathematics is worth having for its own sake, which is a premise these developments do not touch. If anything the case strengthens: when results become cheap and understanding does not, what society is buying when it employs a mathematician is precisely the understanding, and it was arguably buying that all along.

That argument is sound about the aggregate and says nothing about the distribution, which is where the difficulty actually lies. A profession can retain its total funding while the allocation within it shifts drastically, and the mid-tier argument of [the third post](/blog/when-the-proxies-stop-measuring/) is exactly a claim about allocation. The premise is also political rather than economic, and political premises are not stable: the same period that produced these results produced an institute for reasoning technologies launched under budgetary duress. Cheapness is a reason the profession will not be abolished. It is not a reason any particular person will be employed.

Which of these is worth doing depends on the scenario, and the scenarios are genuinely open. If capability settles near its present level, most of this is unnecessary and existing institutions absorb the change as they absorbed computer algebra. If the formalization factor crosses one within a decade, the maintained object and the funding of libraries become urgent and much of the rest follows from them. If systems become capable at conjecture as well as proof, none of it is sufficient, and the discipline faces a question about purpose rather than about process.

The long-horizon possibilities are worth stating explicitly, since this series has assumed a middle case throughout. At one end, capability stalls, the tools settle into the role computer algebra occupies, and in twenty years this literature reads like the anxieties of 1988. At the other, systems eventually supply taste as well as technique, and mathematics becomes a humanistic discipline pursued because people want to understand, in the way classical scholarship is: a smaller profession, not a dishonourable one, and better named than treated as unthinkable. The intermediate case is both likelier and stranger. Humans direct research programmes, the scarce skill is knowing what to ask and recognising when an answer matters, and everything above about training judgment rather than technique stops being defensive and becomes the curriculum. Nothing in the current evidence adjudicates between these three. Anyone who tells you otherwise is guessing.

## What to watch

A few indicators will resolve the ambiguity faster than the general discourse will.

- *Exposition and attribution in subsequent First Proof batches*, and in particular whether they improve or stay flat while success rates climb. If generation improves and attribution does not, the digestion bottleneck is the whole story.
- *Whether machine contributions migrate into formal artefacts.* If the norm becomes "machine output arrives Lean-checked", the epistemic crisis largely dissolves and only the sociology remains. This indicator moved faster than any other while this series was being written, since the [August results](https://openai.com/index/ten-advances-in-mathematics/) shipped with Lean certificates for every claim. Whether that becomes a norm or remains one company's marketing decision is the open part, and the test is whether the next such announcement, from anyone, is expected to carry certificates and is criticised if it does not.
- *Whether the formal libraries grow faster than new mathematics does*, the crossing Kontorovich identifies as the precondition for everything else, and for which autoformalization projects clearing multi-year human backlogs would be the first visible sign. As a private instrument rather than a public indicator, the formalization factor in your own subfield: the honest test is not whether you could formalise your last theorem, but whether you could *state* it.
- *The backlog of unverified AI submissions on the Erdős problems site*, and whether it gets absorbed, ignored, or institutionalised. It is the smallest visible instance of proof indigestion and will show the shape of the general case first.
- *The first serious retraction* traceable to an unreviewed AI-assisted argument, and whether the community treats it as a scandal about one author or as evidence about a process.
- *Whether any journal or funder actually reweights* toward exposition, refereeing and canonicalisation, since that is the recommendation which costs real money and status.

On the last of those there is one data point already, and it is more encouraging than I expected when I first wrote the sentence. The US National Science Foundation launched an Institute for Computer-Aided Reasoning in Mathematics in late 2025, under budgetary duress, with [Jeremy Avigad](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jeremy_Avigad) as director and a mission of supporting the adoption of reasoning technologies in mathematics; his own [essay on the subject](https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.03684) is a good short statement of the case. That is infrastructure funding rather than a reweighting of prestige, which is the harder thing, but it is not nothing. If nothing further moves within two or three years, the pessimistic reading is the right one.

Several of those indicators are vague as stated, and a post that makes predictions should say what would count as evidence against it. Four of them can be turned into quantities somebody could actually count.

1. *The share of new mathematics arXiv submissions carrying a tool-disclosure statement* of any kind, currently near zero, whose trajectory measures whether disclosure is normalising or being driven underground.
2. *The growth rate of the formal libraries against the growth rate of new mathematics*, the same two rates whose inequality is the precondition for everything, both of them public.
3. *Time-to-first-decision and referee-decline rates* at a handful of general journals, which is where a queueing collapse would show up first and unambiguously.
4. *Whether displacement is scattered across subfields, concentrated in the middle of the distribution, or both.* These test different claims and are not alternatives: patchy thinning uncorrelated with prestige would show that task exposure follows problem shape, while thinning concentrated in the middle regardless of subfield would show the career effect running through how output is valued rather than through what is automatable. Existing bibliometric data can separate them.
5. *The share of papers that are computational constructions rather than conceptual contributions*, which tests the subfield-level Goodhart prediction directly. If constructions become cheap while structure theory does not, that balance should shift measurably within a few years.
6. *Where credit attaches*, readable from acknowledgements, citation practice and prize citations. The migration hypothesis predicts esteem moving toward posing, verifying and explaining; if in ten years it still attaches predominantly to first announcement, that hypothesis failed.
7. *The ratio of incremental strengthenings to genuine reframings* in machine-assisted work within a subfield. The no-error-signal argument predicts a bias toward adding hypotheses and extending results rather than restructuring a framework, since restructuring costs complexity before it repays any.

If disclosure spreads, the library gap narrows, refereeing times hold, displacement shows no pattern of either kind, and credit stays where it is, then the argument of this series was wrong in ways these numbers would have shown.

## Two accounts, and what would settle them

The evidence assembled here supports two readings of the near future. They are usually presented as opposed, and each is usually met in a weakened form: one as vendor enthusiasm, the other as professional denial. Stated at their strongest they are both serious, and neither is currently refuted. Setting them side by side, and being explicit about what would decide between them, is as close to a conclusion as the present evidence permits. The indicators listed above concern the institutional predictions of this series. What follows concerns the capability question those predictions were deliberately made independent of.

*The harvest account.* What has fallen so far is a stock rather than a frontier. Mathematics has accumulated, over a century of framework-building, a large inventory of problems that are stated, unattacked and reachable: the conjectures that fit inside a context window and are provable in the language the subject already has. Machines are unusually well suited to clearing that inventory, since clearing it rewards patience in an unpromising search space rather than insight, and since the results terminate in objects a checker can verify. On this reading the striking events of the past year are a one-off reaping of what humans had sown and not got round to harvesting, and the rate should fall as the inventory empties.

The supporting evidence is substantial, and most of it comes from people running these systems rather than selling them. The measured success rate on the [Erdős problems corpus](https://www.erdosproblems.com) is a percent or two, concentrated at the easy end. DeepMind's own case study records that several problems listed as open were open only in the sense of being hard to find. The First Proof referees found exposition and attribution failing systematically, which is what one would expect if mathematics is being retrieved and recombined rather than understood. Practitioners report three-quarters correctness and systems that do not know what is interesting. Tao observes that these models are worst at precisely the thing graduate training exists to teach, namely extracting from a failed attempt the lesson that reshapes the next one. [Tom Zahavy](https://www.tomzahavy.com/files/llms-cant-jump.pdf) supplies a mechanism for the ceiling, since with no error signal there is no gradient toward restructuring, and the bias therefore runs toward strengthening and patching rather than reframing. And the composition of the August results fits the account closely, because a construction, a counterexample and a bound are exactly the harvestable shapes.

*The capability account.* The constraint is not a stock but general reasoning ability, which is improving quickly and has repeatedly falsified confident statements about what it would not reach. The trajectory is the argument. No points at the Olympiad in 2023, silver in 2024, gold in 2025, saturation in 2026. Plain models solved none of the ten First Proof problems under a thirty-minute budget in April; harnesses solved seven of them in June. In August, problems open for decades fell with certificates attached, at a cost that would not cover a conference trip. A prominent sceptic conceded his bet and said the remainder was a matter of not very much time. On this reading the harvest account is what each of the previous three ceilings also looked like from the inside, and the operative mechanism is general rather than stock-specific: patience and breadth do not run out.

*Why the present evidence does not discriminate.* The two accounts make the same prediction for the coming year. Both expect more impressive results, one because the inventory is not empty and the other because capability keeps rising. They differ only in what happens after that, which is why no further announcement will settle anything. Two asymmetries keep the question open. Every published figure is a filtered sample, so that ten announced results out of an unknown number attempted establishes a lower bound on capability and nothing at all about the rate, and no laboratory discloses a denominator. And the harvest account has a bad habit available to it, which is redescribing each new success as having been harvestable after all. An account that can absorb any outcome has stopped being a hypothesis, and its proponents, including this series where it leans that way, owe a statement in advance of what would count against it.

*What would settle it.* Five things, in rough order of how soon they could be observed.

1. *The composition of the next crop.* The harvest account predicts more of the same shape: constructions, counterexamples and bounds, in areas holding large stocks of stated but unattacked problems. The capability account predicts the composition broadens toward structure theorems, toward universally quantified statements with no witness to hand a checker, and toward results that require a new definition before they can even be stated. This is observable within a year and requires nobody's cooperation.
2. *Returns to effort.* The harvest account predicts diminishing returns as the inventory thins, meaning more agents and more compute for fewer results. The capability account predicts flat or improving returns. [Tobias Osborne](https://tjoresearchnotes.wordpress.com/2026/08/02/reaping-without-sowing/)'s report of matching a far larger effort at five orders of magnitude less compute is a single data point, and it suggests, if anything, how much slack the current stock still holds.
3. *Whether a machine-originated definition is adopted.* This is the sharpest test, and both the harvest account and the abduction argument stake themselves on it. Strengthening a theorem and extending it to one more class are reachable by construction. Producing a definition that other mathematicians take up because it makes a class of problems tractable is what neither account expects. A single clear instance would decide a great deal.
4. *Whether actively contested problems begin to fall.* The long-tail explanation holds that what is being cleared was neglected rather than hard. If problems that strong people were actively attacking and failing at start falling, that explanation is finished. The unit-distance case does not settle it, since the effort there had gone into proving the conjecture rather than into refuting it.
5. *Denominators.* If any laboratory published attempts against successes, most of this section would be unnecessary. That none does is itself informative about the epistemic environment described earlier.

*What follows on either account.* Here is the part that does not depend on the answer, and it is the reason this series has been able to proceed without one. Under the harvest account, generation is cheap for a large and unexhausted stock while verification and digestion remain expensive and unrewarded. Under the capability account the same is true, sooner and across more of the subject. Neither account contains any mechanism that makes refereeing cheaper, that makes exposition count for more, or that stops publication counts from decaying as a signal of anything. The institutional argument is therefore robust in a way the capability forecast is not, and that is the answer to the obvious question of why anyone should act now rather than wait for the picture to clarify. The picture will clarify. It will not clarify in a direction that makes the bottleneck disappear.

What the accounts do determine is how much time there is, and whether the effects on careers arrive as a drift or as a shock. Under the harvest account a subfield gets a few years of disruption and then something recognisable returns, with its stock of reachable problems permanently thinner and the value of a portfolio built from them permanently lower. Under the capability account nothing returns, and what is reorganised is the discipline rather than one part of its output.

*A possibility neither account contains.* Both are accounts of machine capability, and both treat the supply of human mathematicians as a constant. [Jacob Tsimerman](https://x.com/Jacob_Tsimerman/status/2051116022585770170)'s observation cuts directly against that: he does mathematics not only to find truth but because he enjoys the problem solving, and if that disappears, many people may not want to do it any more. If what many people wanted was the problem solving, and the problem solving is what became cheap, then the binding constraint on mathematics could turn out to be human willingness rather than machine capability. That is a variable nobody is measuring, it is not obviously downstream of the capability question, and a discipline can find itself short of people for reasons having nothing to do with how good the tools are.

*What would defeat the argument.* The scenario in which this series is simply wrong deserves stating as plainly as the rest, because it is not remote. The whole argument rests on one asymmetry: generation became cheap and verification did not. If verification also becomes cheap, the argument dissolves. Three things point that way at once. The August results shipped with machine-checkable certificates, which removes the referee from the correctness question entirely for results of that kind. Osborne's proposal to point the same harvesting machinery at review would supply hostile referees, provenance audits and openness sweeps for a few hundred dollars a night. And blueprint tooling points toward proof at multiple resolutions, letting a reader verify the architecture of an argument and descend only where the claims are novel. If those three converge, the digestion bottleneck is transitional rather than structural, and the right response is patience rather than institutional reform. My own reading is that this is not the likeliest outcome, because exposition, canonicalisation and the judgment of what matters are not the same task as checking, and checking is the only one of them being automated. But it is the outcome that would make this series a period piece, and a reader is entitled to know which way that would go.

## Closing

Technologically this is the fourth or fifth wave of one process: a layer of mathematical labour is delegated, a skill atrophies, a mode of discovery opens, the boundary of "routine" moves, and the profession renegotiates what a paper may leave unsaid. We have done this with tables, with the four colour theorem, with validated numerics, and with computer algebra, and each time the alarm was partly justified and the discipline was fine.

Institutionally it is not the same thing at all, for reasons that have little to do with how clever the models are. The tool is stochastic rather than deterministic; it targets judgment rather than manipulation; it is built out of the mathematical commons rather than sold into it; it treats mathematics as an instrument for something else; and it arrives from outside on a timescale that gives the community no veto. Those five properties are why norms are the only available lever, and why the useful question has shifted from *what can it do?* to the question the ICM lecture actually asks: what do we want, now that we can no longer get it as a free by-product of doing something else?

My own reading is that the discipline's real vulnerability is not that a machine will prove the Riemann hypothesis in a way nobody understands. It is the duller scenario in which generation becomes cheap, verification and digestion stay expensive and unrewarded, the proxies by which we assess each other quietly stop measuring anything, and the literature fills with correct, unread, uncanonicalised results. That failure mode is entirely within the community's power to prevent, and preventing it requires no forecast about capabilities at all.

One concession is owed at the end. [The third post in this series](/blog/when-the-proxies-stop-measuring/) predicted that its section on conjecture would be the first thing to date. That was wrong: what dated fastest was the evidence, and it dated within four days of the draft that described it as current. The framework survived the update, and one of its more specific predictions was arguably confirmed by it, which is some comfort but not much.

Three further corrections belong on the same ledger, since a series that scores its own predictions should also record where it changed its mind. The verification-cost claim was first stated in a general form that two historical episodes refute, and it is used throughout in the restricted form that survives them, which applies only where generation has already become cheap. [The first post](/blog/generation-is-hard-checking-is-cheap/) also ran together two questions that need separating, namely when a mathematician adopts a tool and what a community does with a result somebody else produced; the first is Kontorovich's formalization factor, the second is the axis this series actually uses, and an earlier draft conceded the second to the first in error. And the hypothesis of [the fourth post](/blog/where-the-argument-bends/) is confirmed by the August results less cleanly than its first statement implied, since one of the five named results is a universally quantified theorem that fell anyway. The honest position is that anyone writing about this now is writing about a moving object from inside it, and the appropriate posture is neither the confidence of the manifesto nor the detachment of the survey, but something closer to what a good referee report does: state what is established, state what is conjectured, and be explicit about which is which.

## Epilogue

A post of this kind should say where its author stands, so here is my own practice.

I use these tools mainly as a writing assistant for English. The benefit is easy to illustrate: if a good introduction used to take me two weeks, I can now write it in two days. What that buys is not speed for its own sake but attention: I can concentrate on what I want to say instead of struggling with the grammatical rules for expressing it in a language that is not my first. The process is iterative, and the output always needs careful review before it says exactly what I meant, which is the point rather than a caveat. Having handed over that routine, I write papers considerably faster than I used to.

I include this partly because it is the honest answer to the question this series circles, and partly because it happens to instantiate several of the claims made above. The friction removed was artificial in the precise sense set out earlier: it belonged to the medium rather than to the mathematics. Verification is cheap, since I can tell whether a paragraph says what I meant. The judgment stayed where it was, the tool having no view about what an introduction should argue. And the seminar test is passed trivially, because nothing in the paper is a claim I could not defend unaided.

There is a general point inside the personal one, and it belongs on the ledger alongside the concerns above. Writing mathematics in English imposes a tax that falls entirely on those who did not grow up speaking it: weeks per paper, and a persistent disadvantage in how the work is received that has nothing to do with its quality. It is plausibly the largest single inequity in mathematical publishing, and it is one these tools substantially remove. Whatever else turns out to be true, a technology that removes most of that cost, and lets a mathematician working in a second language be read as clearly as anyone else, has done something straightforwardly good.
